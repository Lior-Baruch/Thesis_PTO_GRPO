\section*{Ethics Statement}

No human participants and no clinical data were involved: every conversation in this study is
between two language models, and every persona is synthetic. Nothing here was deployed to, or
evaluated with, people seeking care.

The system studied is a research artifact and is not a therapy tool. We want to be explicit that
this paper's result is a reason for caution rather than reassurance about such systems. A therapist
model trained this way improves on validated-questionnaire scores while acquiring a behaviour ---
flattering the client --- that those questionnaires reward and that MI practice treats as a
failure; and the intervention we tested to remove that behaviour replaced it with unsolicited
advice, a failure mode that overrides client autonomy and is arguably the one MI exists to avoid.
An automated evaluation reporting improvement on the instrument would have missed both.

The broader risk we would flag is the use of LLM-scored validated instruments as a training signal
in mental-health settings without a behavioural audit alongside. Our results suggest such a signal
can be optimised into a policy that scores well and practises badly, and that fixing the specific
bad practice one happens to measure does not fix the underlying incentive.

Compute and API cost for this study were modest by contemporary standards (low hundreds of USD in
inference against hosted models); we report the candidate budgets in \S\ref{sec:setup} so the
comparison's cost asymmetry is auditable rather than implicit.

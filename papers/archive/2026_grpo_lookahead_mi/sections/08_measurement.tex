\section{Where the ruler stops working}
\label{sec:measurement}

The result in \S\ref{sec:reward} rests on two graders agreeing about an ordering. This section
reports, at the level of individual conversations, where they stop agreeing --- because that place
turns out to be the winning checkpoint itself, and a paper that reported the gain without it would
be hiding its own sharpest caveat.

\textbf{Arm-level agreement is strong.} Across the experiment's 22 model states, the held-out
judge preserves the sign of $1{,}640$ of $8 \times \binom{22}{2} = 1{,}848$ pairwise state
$\times$ instrument contrasts ($88.7\%$); restricted to contrasts the training oracle scores at
$|\Delta| \geq 0.25$ this rises to $97.0\%$, and at $|\Delta| \geq 0.50$ to $98.9\%$. The two
graders disagree mainly about differences too small to claim, and every contrast this paper
reports is well inside the range where they agree.

\textbf{Per-conversation agreement collapses at the winning checkpoint.} That statistic is
\emph{arm-level}, and it does not license a per-conversation claim. On the rewarded rubric Q1,
per-conversation agreement between the two graders along the $\Kf$ arm reads $0.941$ at iteration
5, then $0.877$, $0.842$, $0.769$, and falls to $\mathbf{0.487}$ at iteration 9 and
$\mathbf{0.544}$ at iteration 10 --- against a median of $0.841$ across the 22 model states,
making these by far the two lowest-agreeing states on Q1 (the next lowest is $0.744$). The best
checkpoint by score is the checkpoint the two graders most disagree about, conversation by
conversation. (The $\Kf$ arm loses Q1 agreement over its last two iterations while the $\Kz$ arm
never leaves the normal range; with one training run per arm we cannot separate ``this
checkpoint'' from ``long $K{=}5$ training.'')

Nor is the collapse confined to the rewarded rubric, though seeing that requires reading each
instrument against its own baseline --- the eight rubrics differ in baseline agreement by more than
the effect (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:agreement}). Ranked against their own 22-state distributions,
the MITI coder falls furthest ($-0.345$ from its median, its worst state in the experiment), then
Q1 ($-0.297$), Q2 and MICI; the four patient-perspective Likert instruments barely move. The
graders stop agreeing on the \emph{rewarded} and \emph{behaviour-coding} rubrics and keep agreeing
on the global-impression ones --- consistent with two causes stacking: saturation on what was
optimised (below), and the known difficulty of cross-model behaviour coding, where MITI is the
least dependable instrument in the battery (single-grader dependability $0.624$ vs.\
$0.91$--$0.97$ for the Likert rubrics). This is also why \S\ref{sec:behaviour}'s argument rests on
the deterministic lexical marker rather than the MITI/MICI counts.

\textbf{The mechanism is one-sided saturation, not homogenised output.} A correlation can collapse
because the conversations became uniform, or because one rater ran out of range. Here it is the
second, and the two are distinguishable because \textbf{only one of the two graders' spreads
moves} (Figure~\ref{fig:saturation}). The training oracle's Q1 standard deviation falls
monotonically across $\Kf$'s run, $1.336$ at base to $0.701$ at iteration 10 (Spearman
$\rho = -0.86$, $p = .001$) --- a variance ratio of $0.701^2 / 1.336^2 = 0.275$, robust to the
anchor ($0.285$ vs.\ iteration 1, $0.544$ vs.\ the mean of iterations 1--10). It has lost most of
its discriminative range as its scores pile up near the top of the scale. On the \emph{identical
conversations}, the held-out judge's Q1 spread does not move ($\rho = +0.44$, $p = .18$).
Flat is sufficient: had the conversations themselves become homogeneous, \emph{both} spreads would
have shrunk. One shrank and the other did not, on the same 96 conversations, so what changed is the
ruler and not the thing measured.

\textbf{What this does and does not undermine.} Not the headline: the held-out judge, the one
still discriminating, independently ranks $\Kf$ above $\Kz$ at $\dz\,1.030$ --- a larger
standardised effect than the saturating grader reports. It does undermine three things, marked
wherever they appear: any per-conversation claim about $\Kf$'s late checkpoints on Q1, Q2, MITI or
MICI; the absolute endpoint level $4.517$, which sits in the compressed part of the training
oracle's range and should be read as ``near the top of this grader's scale,'' not as a calibrated
score; and the precise magnitude of the residual MI-inconsistency in \S\ref{sec:behaviour-honest}.
No $K{=}5$ model state has a repeatability replicate, so cross-grader agreement on this arm cannot
be benchmarked against a measured attenuation ceiling --- we report the raw correlations and say
so.

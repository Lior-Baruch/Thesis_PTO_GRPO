\section{Look-ahead more than doubles the gain}
\label{sec:reward}

\subsection{The headline}

At the matched iteration-10 endpoint, look-ahead wins on \textbf{every instrument under both
graders} (Table~\ref{tab:endpoint}, appendix). On the rewarded rubric the gap is $+0.765$ ($\dz\,0.905$)
under the training oracle and $+0.616$ ($\dz\,1.030$) under the held-out judge. That the held-out
judge reports a \emph{larger} standardised effect than the grader optimised against is the key
fact here: the gain is not an artifact of the optimisation target.

Measured against each arm's own base, look-ahead more than doubles what the same optimizer extracts
from the same oracle: $+1.554$ versus $+0.686$ on Q1+Q2 under the training oracle
($1.554 / 0.686 = 2.27\times$), and $+1.038$ versus $+0.396$ under the held-out judge
($1.038 / 0.396 = 2.62\times$). In absolute terms the $\Kf$ policy ends at $4.517$ on a 1--5 scale
against the $\Kz$ policy's $3.753$, from statistically indistinguishable bases ($2.963$ and
$3.067$; the base-vs-base contrast is $+0.104$, $\dz\,0.115$, n.s.).

The instruments \emph{outside} the reward move too, which is what distinguishes a genuine gain
from a target-specific one: MI-SAT ($\dz\,0.531$ / $0.829$ under the two graders), CSQ-8
($0.482$ / $0.678$), WAI-SR ($0.513$ / $0.442$), the MITI technique coder ($0.735$ / $0.502$), and
patient change talk ($0.516$ / $0.563$) all favour look-ahead under both graders
(Table~\ref{tab:endpoint}). MI-inconsistent behaviour, the one lower-is-better axis, shows the
largest standardised effect of all ($\dz\,-1.862$ / $-1.567$); \S\ref{sec:behaviour} takes that
apart, because it is where the two graders stop agreeing about magnitude.

\subsection{The effect has an onset}

Look-ahead is not free from the first step, and the trajectory matters for anyone deciding how long
to train. For the first three iterations the two arms are statistically indistinguishable on Q1+Q2
under both graders. The advantage opens at iteration 4, is Holm-significant at 6 of the 10
iterations under each grader (primary: 4, 6--10; held-out: 4--7, 9, 10), and is \emph{largest at
the endpoint} --- there is no sign of it having peaked (Figure~\ref{fig:headline}; the full
by-iteration table is Appendix Table~\ref{tab:byiter}).

Two readings are ruled out by the same figure: this is not $\Kz$ collapsing while $\Kf$ holds
steady ($\Kf$ climbs throughout), nor a single-iteration artifact (the gap is Holm-significant at
every iteration from 6 to 10 under the training oracle). It is not smooth --- most of the final
margin opens between iterations 8 and 9, partly because $\Kz$ falls from its own iteration-8 peak
of $4.082$ to $3.753$; \S\ref{sec:behaviour} identifies what that policy was doing over exactly
those iterations.
\textbf{The endpoint survives a second draw.} Every state here is scored on one 96-conversation
sample, so we re-drew the winning one: 96 fresh conversations from the same $\Kf$ iteration-10
adapter, same personas, same unseeded decoding, scored on all eight instruments by both graders.
That gives a noise floor at a \emph{trained} checkpoint, which the design otherwise lacks: the two
draws of the same policy differ by at most $|\dz|\,0.174$ across 9 instruments and both graders,
nothing significant. And the headline holds --- substituting the replicate, the $\Kf-\Kz$ gap on
Q1+Q2 is $+0.709$ ($\dz\,0.919$) / $+0.637$ ($\dz\,0.949$) against $+0.765$ / $+0.616$ originally:
same sign, same significance, effect sizes within $0.08$. The endpoint \emph{level} moves
$4.517 \rightarrow 4.461$, the honest precision of a single-draw score here. The re-draw covers
the $\Kf$ side only, so it tests the contested endpoint rather than the whole contrast.

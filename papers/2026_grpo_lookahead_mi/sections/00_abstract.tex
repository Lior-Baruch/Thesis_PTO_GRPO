\begin{abstract}
Reinforcement learning from an LLM judge usually scores a candidate turn on the turn itself. In
multi-turn goal-oriented dialogue that is a short-sighted proxy: the turn that looks best in
isolation is not the turn that leads somewhere good. We test the alternative directly in
motivational interviewing (MI), training a Llama-3.2-1B therapist against a simulated patient with
Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) and varying only \emph{what the judge sees} --- the
candidate turn alone ($K{=}0$), or the candidate plus the $K{=}5$-turn continuation that the
current policy and the patient actually produce from it; oracle, group size, temperatures, 96
patient personas, and 10 iterations are matched. Trajectory-level reward more than doubles what
the same optimizer extracts from the same oracle --- gains over base of $+1.554$ vs.\ $+0.686$ on
the rewarded MI rubric under the training oracle, and $+1.038$ vs.\ $+0.396$ under a held-out
judge from a different model family --- and it is the difference between learning MI and learning
to flatter the judge: turn-level reward drives MI-inconsistent behaviour up fourfold, and a
judge-free lexical marker finds over-praise in $67\%$ of the $K{=}0$ policy's therapist turns at
the endpoint, against $6\%$ under $K{=}5$. The cost is real --- a median $1.92\times$ per
optimizer step --- and on a reconstructed GPU-hour axis the lever \emph{loses} below ${\sim}18$
GPU-hours, draws level near $23$, and wins significantly from $35$, so the result is a claim about
a budget, not an iteration count. Two caveats travel with it: under the held-out judge $K{=}5$'s
MI-inconsistency still rises, at under half the $K{=}0$ rate rather than not at all; and the
winning checkpoint has the weakest per-conversation cross-grader agreement in the experiment,
because the trained-against grader saturates one-sidedly, losing three-quarters of its variance on
the rewarded rubric while the held-out judge's spread over the same conversations does not move.
The arm-level conclusion survives both; the per-conversation ruler does not.
\end{abstract}

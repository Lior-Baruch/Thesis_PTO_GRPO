\begin{abstract}
Reinforcement learning from an LLM judge usually scores a candidate turn on the turn itself. In a
multi-turn goal-oriented dialogue that is a short-sighted proxy: the turn that looks best in
isolation is not the turn that leads somewhere good. We test the alternative directly in
motivational interviewing (MI), training a Llama-3.2-1B therapist against a simulated patient with
Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) and varying only \emph{what the judge sees} --- the
candidate turn alone ($K{=}0$), or the candidate plus the $K{=}5$-turn continuation that the
current policy and the patient actually produce from it. Everything else is matched: the same
oracle, group size, minimum context length, temperatures, and 96 patient personas over 10
iterations.

Trajectory-level reward more than doubles what the same optimizer extracts from the same oracle.
Against its own base, the $K{=}5$ arm gains $+1.554$ on the 22-item MI rubric versus $+0.686$ for
$K{=}0$ under the training oracle ($1.554/0.686 = 2.27\times$) and $+1.038$ versus $+0.396$ under a
held-out judge from a different model family ($2.62\times$); at the matched endpoint the gap is
$+0.765$ ($\dz\,0.905$) and $+0.616$ ($\dz\,1.030$) respectively, persona-paired over 96 personas
and significant on all eight instruments under both graders. It is also the difference between
learning MI and learning to flatter the judge: the $K{=}0$ policy's MI-inconsistent behaviour rises
from $0.211$ to $0.838$, driven by over-praise: a judge-free lexical marker finds it in
$67\%$ of the $K{=}0$ policy's therapist turns at the endpoint, against $6\%$ under $K{=}5$.

We report the cost honestly. Look-ahead costs a median $1.92\times$ per optimizer step and
$1.835\times$ over the run, and on a reconstructed GPU-hour axis it \emph{loses} below ${\sim}18$
GPU-hours, drawing level near $23$ and winning significantly from $35$ --- so the result is a
statement about a budget, not about an iteration count. Two caveats travel with it: under the
held-out judge $K{=}5$'s MI-inconsistency still rises, at under half the $K{=}0$ rate rather than
not at all; and the winning checkpoint has the weakest per-conversation cross-grader agreement in
the experiment on the rewarded rubric ($r = 0.544$ there, against a $0.855$ median across all 44
model states), because the training grader saturates one-sidedly --- losing three-quarters of its
variance on that rubric while the held-out judge's spread over the same conversations does not
move. The arm-level conclusion survives both; the per-conversation ruler does not.
\end{abstract}

\begin{abstract}
When an LLM judge supplies the reward for a dialogue policy, it is usually shown the candidate
turn and nothing after it. In multi-turn goal-oriented dialogue that is a short-sighted proxy: the
turn that reads best on its own is often not the turn that leads anywhere good. We present
\textbf{\LAGRPO}: before the judge scores a candidate therapist turn, the patient simulator and the
current policy continue the conversation for $K$ further turns, and the judge scores the
continuation. The idea comes from preference-tree optimisation for motivational interviewing (MI);
we move it to Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), where the reward horizon reweights every
sampled candidate rather than selecting a preference pair. In a controlled pair of runs
(a Llama-3.2-1B therapist against simulated patients, 96 personas, 10 iterations, identical in
every setting but the horizon), $K{=}5$ more than doubles what GRPO extracts from the same judge:
$+1.55$ vs.\ $+0.69$ rubric points over base under the training oracle and $+1.04$ vs.\ $+0.40$
under a held-out judge from another model family, ahead on all eight evaluation instruments under
both graders, and replicated on a fresh evaluation draw. It also changes what is learned.
Turn-level reward teaches flattery: MI-inconsistent behaviour quadruples, and a judge-free lexical
marker finds over-praise in $67\%$ of the $K{=}0$ policy's therapist turns at the endpoint against
$6\%$ under $K{=}5$, which asks more questions instead. Two caveats travel with the result. Under
the held-out judge, $K{=}5$'s MI-inconsistency still rises, at under half the $K{=}0$ rate; and at
the best checkpoint the training oracle saturates one-sidedly, losing three-quarters of its
per-conversation variance on the rewarded rubric while the held-out judge's spread over the same
conversations does not move. The arm-level ordering survives both; the per-conversation scores at
that checkpoint do not.
\end{abstract}

\section{Discussion and conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{The horizon of the reward selects which behaviour pays.} The $K{=}0$ arm finds unearned
praise, whose payoff is immediate, in the patient-perspective rubric the judge fills in for the
turn it is shown, and whose cost is deferred: a patient met with reassurance instead of a
reflection produces sustain talk over the following turns, which the judge never sees. The
$K{=}5$ arm cannot profit from it, because the cost now lands inside the window the judge scores,
and the behaviour that pays there is the one MI prescribes: reflecting the patient's own arguments
for change, which the patient then elaborates. Extending the horizon does not remove reward
hacking (the look-ahead policy carries a persuasion residue, and the held-out judge sees more
praise in it than the training oracle does); it changes which behaviour pays. How the horizon
produces that difference inside the update we could not isolate: three candidate mechanisms were
testable and none is confirmed (Appendix~\ref{app:mechanism}); what remains is a small, repeated
difference in which sibling the group prefers, compounded over ten iterations of a self-training
loop, and the process codes show what it amounts to.
\label{sec:mechanism}

\textbf{Conclusion.} For a group-relative optimiser in multi-turn dialogue, what the judge is
shown is a first-order design choice. Scoring the continuation rather than the turn raised what
the same optimiser extracted from the same oracle, and it decided what kind of therapist the
policy became: one that praises whatever the patient said, or one that reflects the patient's own
case for change and is answered with more of it. We recommend reporting the reward horizon as a
hyperparameter, coding the process as well as the outcome, and keeping a second grader with
headroom (Appendix~\ref{app:saturation}).

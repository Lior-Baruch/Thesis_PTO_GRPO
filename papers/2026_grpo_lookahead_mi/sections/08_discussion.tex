\section{Discussion and conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{The horizon of the reward selects the reward hack.} The $K{=}0$ arm finds unearned
praise, whose payoff is immediate and
whose cost is deferred. The $K{=}5$ arm cannot profit from it, because the cost now lands inside
the window the judge sees; it moves toward asking questions and, to a smaller degree, toward the
righting reflex of pushing the patient. Extending the horizon does not remove reward hacking; it
changes which behaviour pays.

\textbf{What we could not isolate.}
\label{sec:mechanism}
Three mechanisms were testable in this design, and none is confirmed (Appendix~\ref{app:mechanism}).
Pooled over the run, the $\Kf$ reward ranks candidates slightly more like the full-session score
does (pairwise rank agreement $0.909$ vs.\ $0.873$ under the training oracle), but the
iteration-level test does not clear $.05$ and at the one matched-policy cut (iteration 1) both
deltas straddle zero. Look-ahead widens the best-minus-worst margin within a
group ($1.300\times$), but the within-group standard deviation grows by the same factor
($1.293\times$), a rescaling the advantage normalisation absorbs: look-ahead changes
\emph{which} candidate the group prefers, not how confidently. What remains is a small, repeated difference in which sibling is preferred,
compounded over ten iterations of a loop that regenerates its training data from the policy it
just updated; isolating it needs the horizon swapped mid-run on one policy.

\textbf{Scope: one optimiser, one regime.} The lever's origin is a preference-pair method:
\citet{baruch2025pto} introduced it with preference trees and DPO in an easier regime of this
task (a 7B policy, more cooperative patients, a weaker judge); whether it helps other optimiser
families in other regimes is outside this paper's scope. Evidence that would overturn it: a
second \emph{training} run
reversing the ordering, an intermediate $K$ showing a threshold rather than a horizon, or a
human MI coder disagreeing with the over-praise finding.

\textbf{Conclusion.} For a group-relative optimiser in multi-turn dialogue, what the judge is
shown is a first-order design choice. Scoring the continuation rather than the turn raised what
the same optimiser extracted from the same oracle, and it decided which failure mode the policy
learned. An LLM judge optimised against also saturates, its scores moving to the top of a
bounded scale where they stop ranking, unseen by arm-level agreement. We recommend reporting
the reward horizon as a hyperparameter, tracking the training grader's variance and ceiling
share, and keeping a second grader with headroom.

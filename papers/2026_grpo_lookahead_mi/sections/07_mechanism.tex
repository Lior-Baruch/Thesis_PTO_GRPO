\section{Why it might work --- and what we could not show}
\label{sec:mechanism}

This section is deliberately weaker than the three before it, and it reports a negative result
about our own preferred explanation. The obvious mechanism for look-ahead is that it makes the
training reward a better proxy for the session outcome. We can measure that proxy quality directly.
It does improve over the run --- and the one cut that could establish the mechanism does not
support it.

\subsection{The faithfulness statistic}

The training reward scores a \emph{partial} conversation; the evaluation scores the whole session.
The gap between them is measurable as pairwise rank agreement: among candidate turns compared
within one (arm, iteration, conversation-length) cell, how often does the training reward order
them the way the full-session score eventually does? Chance is $0.5$; intervals are
conversation-level cluster bootstraps.

\subsection{Over the run, the $\Kf$ signal is more faithful}

Pooled over the ten iterations both arms reached, agreement is $0.909$ $[0.900, 0.917]$ under $\Kf$
against $0.873$ $[0.861, 0.884]$ under $\Kz$ on the training oracle, and $0.800$ against $0.747$ on
the held-out judge; both differences have intervals excluding zero, and $\Kf$ is the more faithful
arm at 7 of 10 iterations under both graders.

\textbf{Two qualifications travel with that number.} First, the iteration-level Wilcoxon test ---
with the ten iterations, rather than the branch pairs, as the unit of observation --- does
\emph{not} clear $.05$ ($p = .084$ primary, $p = .193$ held out). The pooled interval treats branch
pairs as independent and is the more permissive of the two, so we describe the difference as small
and consistent rather than significant. Second, and more importantly, at iteration $n$ the two arms
are \emph{different policies}, generating \emph{different conversations}. This comparison therefore
measures a joint property of the scoring rule and of the trajectory the scoring rule put the policy
on. It cannot separate them, and so it cannot establish a mechanism.

\subsection{At a matched policy, the effect disappears}

There is exactly one cut in this design where the scoring rule is close to the only difference: the
first iteration, where both arms start from the same base policy and differ only in whether five
simulated turns were appended before the oracle saw each candidate.

At that cut, \textbf{nothing is detectable}. All four deltas straddle zero (training oracle: $+0.015$
$[-0.026, 0.059]$ for GRPO; held-out judge: $-0.014$ $[-0.075, 0.048]$), and the per-length-bin
breakdown points in \emph{opposite directions on the two graders} --- 17 of 20 bins favour $\Kz$
under the training oracle, 17 of 20 favour $\Kf$ under the held-out judge. A sign that flips with
the grader is the shape of no effect, not of a small one.

We therefore report the proxy-quality mechanism as \textbf{unsupported}. Look-ahead's benefit is
not explained by the $K{=}5$ reward being a better-calibrated ranker of candidate turns at a fixed
policy. The matched-policy cut is also the weakest-powered one --- a single model state per arm,
and only approximately matched, since the policy drifts within the iteration under $K$-specific
rewards --- so this is an absence of evidence rather than evidence of absence. It is not, however,
a result we can quietly omit while quoting the pooled row.

\subsection{Nor does it work by sharpening the group}

A second candidate mechanism is that look-ahead spreads the eight siblings' rewards further apart,
better conditioning the group-relative advantage. It does spread them: the mean best-minus-worst
margin within a group is $1.300\times$ larger under $\Kf$ $([1.275, 1.326])$. But the within-group
standard deviation grows by almost exactly the same factor $(1.293\times, [1.267, 1.317])$, and
GRPO divides the advantage by that standard deviation. The ratio of the two ratios is $1.006$
$([1.002, 1.010])$ --- a pure rescaling, which the advantage normalisation absorbs.

Look-ahead changes \emph{which} candidate the group prefers, not how confidently it prefers it.

One iteration inverts the level of this, and it is worth stating rather than burying in the pooled
row. At training iteration 10 the margin ratio drops to $0.679$ --- $\Kf$'s within-group margins
are \emph{smaller} than $\Kz$'s there. Both sides move: $\Kz$'s mean margin jumps from $0.248$ to
$0.339$ while $\Kf$'s falls from $0.268$ to $0.230$. The $\Kz$ jump coincides exactly with the
iteration in which its over-praise rate explodes (§\ref{sec:behaviour}: the judge-free marker goes
from $0.093$ to $0.671$ between iterations 9 and 10), which is what a group containing both
flattering and non-flattering candidates would do to a grader that rewards flattery --- the pool
splits and the best-worst gap widens. The $\Kf$ decline is the mirror image: a converging policy
whose eight samples the oracle scores increasingly alike.

Crucially this does \emph{not} break the rescaling result. At that same iteration the
ratio-of-ratios is $0.964$ --- margin and standard deviation still move together, as they do at
every other iteration. What inverts is the level of dispersion in each arm, not the coupling
between spread and margin that the advantage normalisation absorbs. We offer the over-praise
account as consistent with the timing, not as demonstrated: a single iteration, and we have no
manipulation that separates the two.

\subsection{Nor by pushing somewhere else}

The third candidate is the most intuitive: perhaps look-ahead simply points the update in a
different direction. It does not, or at least not much. Writing each iteration's update direction
as the advantage-weighted sum of its candidates' sentence embeddings,
$\operatorname{normalise}(\sum_g w_g \cdot \mathrm{emb}(t_g))$ with $w_g$ the standardised
group-relative advantage that actually scales each completion's gradient, the two arms' pooled
directions have a cosine of $0.804$ --- $0.851$ once divided by the attenuation ceiling that two
split-half-noisy estimates can reach at all. The $\Kz$ and $\Kf$ policies are pushed along
substantially the \emph{same} semantic direction.

That is the sharpest form of the puzzle this paper leaves open. The same optimizer, pushed in
nearly the same direction, with a reward that is not measurably better conditioned and not
measurably more faithful at a fixed policy, nevertheless ends more than twice as far from base.
What differs is \emph{which} of eight siblings is preferred at each step --- a small, repeated
difference in selection, compounded over ten iterations of a loop that regenerates its own training
data from the policy it just updated. We can show the compounding in the outcome
(§\ref{sec:reward}) and in the behaviour (§\ref{sec:behaviour}); we cannot, with this design,
isolate it.

\subsection{What is left}

The honest position is that we have measured \emph{what} look-ahead changes --- the reward's
horizon, and through it the behaviour the policy converges on (§\ref{sec:behaviour}) --- without
isolating the pathway from the first to the second. The two mechanisms we could test are the two we
ruled out. What remains, and what this design cannot reach, is the possibility that the benefit
accrues through the trajectory of policies the lever induces rather than through any fixed-policy
property of the reward. Testing that needs a cut this experiment does not contain: the same policy
trained under both rewards for several iterations with the reward horizon swapped mid-run.

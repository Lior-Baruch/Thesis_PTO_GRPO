\section{GRPO with look-ahead}
\label{sec:method}

Throughout, a \emph{turn} is one utterance by either speaker, a \emph{prefix} $c$ is a transcript
ending on a patient turn, and $\pi_n$ is the therapist policy at the start of training
iteration~$n$. The patient simulator $P$ and the oracle $O$ are fixed LLMs; $O$ returns a scalar
MI score for a transcript.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.86\columnwidth]{figures/method_grpo_group.png}
\caption{One GRPO group under look-ahead. The policy samples $G{=}8$ completions of a prompt; each
is rolled forward $K$ turns by the patient simulator and the current policy; the oracle scores the
extended transcript; the scores are standardised within the group and every completion carries a
gradient. $K{=}0$ removes the rollout and recovers standard GRPO.}
\label{fig:schematic}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The iterative loop.} Training is iterative self-play. At iteration $n$, $\pi_n$ holds 96
conversations with $P$, one per patient persona. Each conversation is sliced after every patient
turn whose prefix has at least MCL utterances (MCL${=}12$ here) to give a set of training prompts.
GRPO updates $\pi_n$ on those prompts for two epochs, the adapter is saved as $\pi_{n+1}$, and the
loop repeats with fresh conversations. The conversations generated at the start of iteration $n$
double as the evaluation set of $\pi_n$; a generate-only pass after the last update evaluates the
final policy, so ten iterations yield eleven evaluated model states per run.

\textbf{The group.} For each prompt $c$, GRPO samples $G{=}8$ completions $t_1,\dots,t_G$ from the
current policy, scores each with a reward $r_g$, and forms the group-relative advantage
$A_g = (r_g - \operatorname{mean}_g r)/\operatorname{std}_g r$. The update is the PPO-clipped policy
gradient on $A_g$ with a KL penalty toward the iteration-start policy. Every completion contributes
a gradient, weighted by how it ranks against its seven siblings.

\textbf{The look-ahead reward.} The only thing we change is the transcript the oracle scores when
computing $r_g$ (Figure~\ref{fig:schematic}):
\[
r_g =
\begin{cases}
O(c + t_g) & K = 0\\[2pt]
O\big(c + t_g + \underbrace{P + \pi_n + \cdots}_{K\ \text{turns}}\big) & K > 0.
\end{cases}
\]
At $K{=}5$ the patient replies to the candidate, the current policy replies to that, and so on for
five further turns; the oracle scores the whole extended transcript, and that score is the
candidate's reward. Because the rollout uses the current policy, a candidate is credited for where
it leads under the therapist that will actually follow it. The loss, the advantage, the KL penalty,
the prompts, and the number of gradient steps are identical across $K$; the horizon changes only
the evidence on which a candidate is ranked against its siblings. Where PTO used the look-ahead
scores to pick one chosen and one rejected turn per branch point, here all eight scores enter the
update.

\textbf{What the lever costs.} Each $K{=}5$ reward adds three patient-simulator calls and two
policy generations to the single oracle call both arms share, so oracle calls are matched across
$K$ by construction while the look-ahead run makes about $393$k extra patient calls and its
optimizer steps take a median $1.92\times$ as long (Limitations). All comparisons are at matched
iteration count, not matched cost.

\textbf{What the rollout actually observed.} A simulated session can end inside the look-ahead
window, most often because the patient closes the conversation. Over every $K{=}5$ candidate of
the run, $82\%$ of rollouts reached the full five turns (mean realised length $4.4$); the
early-ending share ranged from $12\%$ at iteration 10 to $30\%$ at iteration 6, and in $16\%$ of
candidates the patient closed. Early-ending candidates scored at or below their group mean (by up
to $0.09$ points, depending on how the rollout ended) and were the group's best less often than
chance ($0.10$ vs.\ $0.125$). The look-ahead reward thus carries a mild, built-in pressure to keep
the session open (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:tails}).

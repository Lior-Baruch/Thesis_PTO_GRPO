\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Task, simulator and oracle}
\label{sec:task}

A session is a text conversation between a therapist policy and
a simulated patient who is ambivalent about a health behaviour. The patient is simulated by
\texttt{gpt-4o-mini} conditioned on one of the \textbf{96} persona system prompts introduced by
\citet{yosef2024assessing} and used in \citet{baruch2025pto}, the exhaustive
enumeration of $2\ \text{gender} \times 3\ \text{cooperation} \times 2\ \text{problem} \times
2\ \text{duration} \times 2\ \text{prior attempts} \times 2\ \text{age}$
(Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}). A session runs until the patient closes it or a cap of 49 utterances
after the scripted opening is reached; most close earlier, and the untrained therapist's sessions
average 28.5 utterances. The therapist is based on \texttt{Llama-3.2-1B} under a fixed counsellor system prompt,
trained with LoRA adapters, responses capped at 200 tokens (all setting parameters in
Table~\ref{tab:config}). The oracle is the same \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} with JSON-schema-constrained
output; the \emph{training} reward is the mean of two of the evaluation instruments, Q1 and Q2
(\S\ref{sec:setup}). We train on these two because they were built and validated for this use:
\citet{yosef2024assessing} introduced them as LLM-evaluator questionnaires for simulated MI sessions
and showed that the model's ratings are reliable and separate levels of therapist expertise. The
same reward trained the predecessor PTO policy \citep{baruch2025pto}, and the six other instruments
stay outside it, so they measure outcomes the policy was never optimised for.

\textbf{Why MI.} MI's constructs are defined by the patient's response rather than by the
counsellor's utterance. Its target outcome is \emph{change talk}, the patient's own statements in
favour of change (``I guess it would feel like a relief not to be chained to it''), as against
\emph{sustain talk}, statements in favour of the status quo (``I like my routine''; both from
Appendix~\ref{app:example}), and the MITI coding scheme rates a session on cultivating change talk
and softening sustain talk, properties of the trajectory rather than of any one turn
\citep{miller2013mi, moyers2016miti}. The value of a therapist turn is therefore inherently
delayed, which is what a reward horizon is meant to capture. The counsellor's tools are open
questions, \emph{affirmations} (comments on a specific strength or effort of the patient) and
\emph{reflections}, which restate what the patient said (\emph{simple}) or add meaning to it, such
as an inferred feeling (\emph{complex}); the field supplies validated instruments and a tradition
of coding every utterance and asking what follows it.

\subsection{GRPO with look-ahead}
\label{sec:lagrpo}

Throughout, a \emph{turn} is one utterance by either speaker, a \emph{prefix} $c$ is a transcript
ending on a patient turn, $\oplus$ appends turns to a transcript, $\pi_n$ is the therapist policy
after $n$ training iterations ($\pi_0$ is the untrained model, which we call the \emph{Base}), and
$\pi$ is the policy being updated within an iteration ($\pi = \pi_n$ at its start). The patient simulator $P$ and the oracle
$O$ are fixed LLMs; $O$ returns a scalar MI score for a transcript. Algorithm~\ref{alg:loop}
states the procedure; Figure~\ref{fig:schematic} shows one group.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{figures/method_grpo_group.png}
\caption{One GRPO group under look-ahead. The policy samples $G{=}8$ completions of a prompt; each
is rolled forward $K$ turns by the patient simulator $P$ and the policy $\pi$ being trained; the
oracle scores the extended transcript; the scores are standardised within the group and every
completion carries a gradient. At $K{=}0$ the rollout is skipped, the oracle scores the candidate
alone, and standard GRPO is recovered.}
\label{fig:schematic}
\end{figure*}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\LAGRPO\ (one run)}
\label{alg:loop}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE the Base $\pi_0$; simulator $P$; oracle $O$; horizon $K$; group size $G$; MCL;
  iterations $N$
\FOR{$n = 0, \dots, N-1$}
  \STATE $\mathcal{C} \gets$ 96 conversations of $\pi_n$ with $P$ \COMMENT{eval set of $\pi_n$}
  \STATE $\mathcal{D} \gets$ patient-ending prefixes of $\mathcal{C}$ with $\geq$ MCL turns
  \STATE $\pi \gets \pi_n$
  \FOR{each batch of 16 prompts $c \in \mathcal{D}$ (two epochs)}
    \STATE $t_1, \dots, t_G \sim \pi(\cdot \mid c)$ for each $c$
    \STATE $r_g \gets O\big(c \oplus t_g \oplus \tau_K(c \oplus t_g)\big)$ for all $g$
      \COMMENT{Eq.~\ref{eq:reward}}
    \STATE $A_g \gets (r_g - \operatorname{mean}_g r)/\operatorname{std}_g r$ within each group
    \STATE one step on Eq.~\ref{eq:grpo} over the batch, KL to $\pi_n$
  \ENDFOR
  \STATE $\pi_{n+1} \gets \pi$
\ENDFOR
\STATE 96 conversations of $\pi_N$ with $P$ \COMMENT{eval set of $\pi_N$}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\textbf{The iterative loop.} Each training iteration starts from the current policy $\pi_n$,
which holds 96 conversations with $P$, one per patient persona; each conversation is sliced after
every patient turn whose prefix has at least MCL utterances (the \emph{minimum context length}, 12
here; see below) to form the training prompts. For each prompt GRPO samples $G{=}8$ completions and
scores them; the policy takes one optimiser step per batch of 16 prompts (128 completions) on the
group-standardised scores, and after two epochs it is saved as $\pi_{n+1}$ and the loop repeats
with fresh conversations. The conversations that start an iteration double as the evaluation set
of $\pi_n$, and a generate-only pass after the last update evaluates the final policy, so every
policy from the Base $\pi_0$ to $\pi_{10}$ is evaluated; in the figures, iteration $n$ is $\pi_n$.

\textbf{The look-ahead reward.} The two runs differ in one thing: whether a candidate turn is
rolled forward by a simulated $K$-turn look-ahead before it is scored, and hence which transcript
the oracle rewards. Let $\tau_K(x)$ be the $K$-turn rollout of a transcript $x$: $P$ replies, then
$\pi$, then $P$, and so on for $K$ turns, stopping early if the patient closes the session;
$\tau_0(x)$ is empty. Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reward}
r_g \;=\; O\big(c \oplus t_g \oplus \tau_K(c \oplus t_g)\big).
\end{equation}
At $K{=}0$ the oracle scores the candidate alone. At $K{=}5$ it scores the candidate together with
the five turns that followed it under the current policy, so a candidate is credited for where it
leads under the therapist that will actually follow it. A rollout is one sample from the
distribution of continuations, so $r_g$ is a one-sample Monte Carlo estimate of the candidate's
downstream value, with sampling noise from $P$ and $\pi$ added to the oracle's own; GRPO trains on
all $G$ candidates, so that variance enters every advantage.

\textbf{The update.} The step maximises the GRPO objective of \citet{shao2024deepseekmath}: the
per-token surrogate
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:grpo}
\ell_{g,i} = \min(\rho_{g,i} A_g,\,\bar{\rho}_{g,i} A_g) - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}_{g,i},
\end{equation}
averaged over the tokens of each completion and then over the group, where $\rho_{g,i}$ is the
$i$-th token's probability ratio between $\pi$ and the sampling policy, $\bar{\rho}_{g,i}$ its
clip to $[1{-}\epsilon, 1{+}\epsilon]$, and $\mathrm{KL}_{g,i}$ the per-token KL estimate to
$\pi_n$. Both runs make one update per sampled batch
(Table~\ref{tab:config}), so $\rho_{g,i}{=}1$ at the update, the clipping is inactive, and the
step is the advantage-weighted policy gradient over the group with the KL penalty. The loss, the
advantage normalisation, the KL penalty and the prompt-construction rule are identical across
$K$; the horizon enters only through $r_g$.

\textbf{Minimum context length.} Training prompts are prefixes, so the oracle scores a partial
conversation while the evaluation scores a whole session. In a pilot on an earlier configuration
of this task the oracle's ranking of very short prefixes agreed poorly with its ranking of the
completed sessions and recovered from roughly ten utterances, so both runs discard prefixes
shorter than MCL${=}12$; at that minimum the training reward ranks the conversations the prefixes
come from as their full-session scores do in $86$--$89\%$ of pairs, and the agreement rises
slightly with prefix length (Appendix~\ref{app:faithfulness}, Figure~\ref{fig:faithfulness}).

\subsection{Evaluation design}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Instruments.} Evaluation uses eight instruments, each administered by a grader LLM shown
the full transcript (items and prompts in Appendix~\ref{app:instruments}). Q1 and Q2 are the
LLM-evaluator questionnaires of \citet{yosef2024assessing}, 5 and 17 patient-perspective Likert
items on session satisfaction and on alliance and relational communication, shown there to
separate levels of therapist expertise. WAI-SR \citep{hatcher2006waisr} and
CSQ-8 \citep{larsen1979csq} are published patient-report instruments, and MI-SAT is a six-item
intervention-satisfaction survey adapted for this task. Three coders count behaviour: a
MITI~4.2.1-style coder \citep{moyers2016miti} whose four global ratings are averaged; a patient
change-talk coder (PCT; the share of change talk among change and sustain talk, the patient-side
outcome MI aims at); and an MI-inconsistent-behaviour coder (MICI; coded acts per therapist turn,
lower is better). \QQ\ is thus both the optimisation target and a reported outcome
(Limitations); the six instruments outside the reward and a held-out judge (below) are the checks
on it.

\textbf{The utterance coder.} The eight instruments are session-level aggregates. For
\S\ref{sec:therapist}--\ref{sec:patient} we also label every utterance: an LLM \emph{utterance
coder} assigns one code to every therapist utterance, its dominant function in MITI and MISC terms
(open question, closed question, simple reflection, complex reflection, affirmation, non-specific
praise, giving information, persuasion, seeking collaboration, confront, other), and one code to
every patient utterance (change talk, sustain talk, neutral), in transcript order
(Appendix~\ref{app:coder}). Affirmation follows the MITI definition, a comment on a specific client
strength or effort, and non-specific praise is coded separately, the distinction the MITI manual
draws when it lists ``I am really proud of you'' as not an affirmation. We count open questions,
reflections, affirmations and seeking collaboration as MI-adherent, and non-specific praise,
persuasion and confrontation as MI-inconsistent. Because the codes are in transcript order, we can
read, for every therapist utterance, what the patient said in reply, and follow how a session
develops.

\textbf{Judges.} Two LLMs score the conversations, and each does two jobs: it fills in the eight
instruments for a whole conversation, and it runs the utterance coder. The \textbf{training
oracle} (\primaryjudge, which also plays the patient and gave the training reward) is the main
judge: the results in the body are its readings. The \textbf{held-out judge} (\heldoutjudge, a
different model family that never touched training) re-scores everything as a check; the body
reports it in Table~\ref{tab:endpoint} and in one sentence per results section, and the rest is in
Appendix~\ref{app:tables}.

\textbf{The two runs.} The runs are a controlled pair: their tracked run configurations differ in
exactly two fields, the look-ahead depth and its batching size (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).
Everything else, from $G{=}8$, KL $\beta{=}0.01$ and the sampling temperature to the seed, the
oracle and the persona set, is identical (Table~\ref{tab:config}). We name them by their horizon,
\Kz\ (turn-level reward) and \Kf\ (look-ahead reward); from \S\ref{sec:reward} on, ``the \Kf\
policy'' is the therapist a run produces and ``the \Kf\ run'' the training that produced it.

\textbf{Evaluation and statistics.} Every policy is scored on all eight instruments and coded
utterance by utterance for all 96 personas by both judges. All contrasts are
\textbf{persona-paired} (personas are reshuffled every iteration, so pairing is on the recovered
persona identity), with Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, Cohen's \dz, bootstrap intervals, and Holm
correction for multiple comparisons within each family of tests. We call a contrast
\emph{significant} when its Holm-corrected $p$ is below .05; a contrast repeated at every
iteration is corrected across the ten iterations, and the rows of Table~\ref{tab:endpoint} across
its nine rows. The two judges' levels are not comparable (on \QQ\ the held-out judge scores
1.2--1.8 points lower), so only contrasts are compared across them, and the two are never
averaged.

\subsection{The faithfulness statistic}
\label{app:faithfulness}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.94\textwidth]{figures/faithfulness_grpo.png}
\caption{Faithfulness by prefix length on the Base's 192 conversations: the share of conversation
pairs, compared within one Base draw at one prefix length, that a score of the prefix orders the
way the full-session score does (pairs tied on either score dropped; chance is $0.5$, below the
axis). The full-session score is the \QQ\ of the whole conversation by \textbf{(a)} the training
oracle and \textbf{(b)} the held-out judge, so (b) also reflects how far the two judges disagree.
Grey triangles: the training oracle's \QQ\ of the prefix alone, at every patient turn from two
utterances. Circles and squares: the training reward at the first training iteration, whose
prompts are prefixes of these conversations (the oracle's \QQ\ for the best of eight candidates,
with the five simulated turns under $\Kf$; Eq.~\ref{eq:reward}); it starts at MCL${=}12$, since
shorter prefixes (shaded) are never trained on. Fewer sessions reach the longer prefixes, and at
50 utterances, the session cap, a prefix can be the whole session. Bands: $95\%$
conversation-level cluster-bootstrap intervals. The training reward pooled over the ten iterations
is described in the text.}
\label{fig:faithfulness}
\end{figure*}

The training reward scores a \emph{partial} conversation; the evaluation scores the whole session.
The gap between them is measurable as pairwise rank agreement: among pairs of conversations
compared within one (run, iteration, prefix-length) cell, how often does the training reward at
that prefix order them the way their full-session scores do? Chance is $0.5$; intervals are
$95\%$ conversation-level cluster bootstraps.

Pooled over the ten iterations, agreement is $0.909$ $[0.900, 0.917]$ under $\Kf$ against $0.873$
$[0.861, 0.884]$ under $\Kz$ on the training oracle, and $0.800$ against $0.747$ on the held-out
judge; both differences have intervals excluding zero, and $\Kf$ is the more faithful run at 7 of
10 iterations under both judges. By prefix length, pooled over the ten iterations, agreement at the
shortest admitted prefix (12 utterances) is $0.860$ under $\Kz$ and $0.886$ under $\Kf$ on the
training oracle, and it does not fall with length ($0.897$ and $0.936$ at 50 utterances). Two
qualifications travel with the difference between the runs. First, the
iteration-level Wilcoxon test, with the ten iterations rather than the conversations as the unit of
observation, does \emph{not} clear $.05$ ($p = .084$ training oracle, $p = .193$ held out). The
pooled interval resamples conversations within each iteration and so ignores the variation between
iterations; it is the more permissive of the two, so we
describe the difference as small and consistent rather than significant. Second, at iteration $n$
the two runs are \emph{different policies} generating \emph{different conversations}, so this
comparison measures a joint property of the scoring rule and of the trajectory the scoring rule put
the policy on; it cannot separate them.

To see what MCL removes, we also scored, with the training oracle, every prefix of the Base's 192
conversations that ends on a patient turn, from two utterances on (2{,}722 prefixes; the prefix
alone, with no candidate and no look-ahead). Its agreement with the training oracle's full-session
score rises from $0.735$ $[0.690, 0.774]$ at two utterances to $0.803$ at ten and $0.846$ at
twelve, and reaches $0.976$ at fifty, where a prefix can be the whole session; with the held-out
judge's full-session score it rises from $0.688$ to $0.782$ at twelve and $0.838$ at fifty
(Figure~\ref{fig:faithfulness}). From twelve utterances on, the first iteration's training reward,
whose prompts are prefixes of the same conversations, follows the prefix alone closely ($0.846$
under $\Kz$ and $0.831$ under $\Kf$ at twelve, $0.977$ and $0.963$ at fifty, training oracle): at
the Base, choosing the best of eight candidates and adding the five simulated turns barely change
how well the training oracle's reward ranks conversations.

\subsection{At a matched policy, the effect disappears}

There is exactly one cut in this design where the scoring rule is close to the only difference: the
first iteration, where both runs start from the same Base policy and differ only in whether five
simulated turns were appended before the oracle saw each candidate. At that cut nothing is
detectable. Both deltas straddle zero ($\Kf - \Kz$ agreement, training oracle: $-0.015$
$[-0.059, 0.026]$; held-out judge: $+0.014$ $[-0.048, 0.075]$), and the per-length-bin breakdown
points in opposite directions on the two judges: 17 of 20 bins favor $\Kz$ under the training
oracle, 17 of 20 favor $\Kf$ under the held-out judge. An effect whose sign depends on the judge
is not distinguishable from zero. The matched-policy cut is also the weakest-powered one (a single
model state per run, and only approximately matched, since the policy drifts within the iteration
under $K$-specific rewards), so this is an absence of evidence rather than evidence of absence; we
report it alongside the pooled estimate.

\subsection{Dispersion, and the iteration-10 inversion}

The mean best-minus-worst margin within a group is $1.300\times$ larger under $\Kf$
$[1.275, 1.326]$; the within-group standard deviation grows by $1.293\times$ $[1.267, 1.317]$; the
ratio of the two is $1.006$ $[1.002, 1.010]$, a pure rescaling that the advantage normalization
absorbs.

One iteration inverts the \emph{level} of this. At training iteration 10 the margin ratio drops to
$0.679$: $\Kf$'s within-group margins are smaller than $\Kz$'s there. Both sides move. $\Kz$'s mean
margin jumps from $0.248$ to $0.339$ while $\Kf$'s falls from $0.268$ to $0.230$. The $\Kz$ jump
coincides with the iteration in which its over-praise rate jumps (the keyword marker goes
from $0.094$ to $0.671$ between iterations 9 and 10), which is what a group containing both
over-praising and non-over-praising candidates would do under a judge that rewards praise: the
pool splits and the best--worst gap widens. The $\Kf$ decline is the mirror image, a converging policy
whose eight samples the oracle scores increasingly alike. This does not break the rescaling result:
at that same iteration the ratio of ratios is $0.964$, so margin and standard deviation still move
together. We offer the over-praise account as consistent with the timing, not as demonstrated.

The trainer logged, at every optimizer step, the per-token KL estimate between the policy and the

\subsection{How much the training reward favors praise}
\label{app:premium}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{figures/praise_premium_grpo.png}
\caption{\textbf{(a)} How much higher each run's own training reward scores praising replies
among the candidates it sampled ($\Kf$: with the five simulated turns that followed the reply): in
each group of eight candidates that holds both a reply containing one of the keyword marker's ten
phrases (Appendix~\ref{app:marker}; matched in the reply only) and one without, the mean

\textbf{(b)} The share of groups that hold both kinds.}
\label{fig:premium}
\end{figure}

The keyword marker (Appendix~\ref{app:marker}) lets us ask whether the training reward itself favors
praise, separately from how often the policy praises (Figure~\ref{fig:premium}). Within each
group of eight candidates we z-score the rewards, and in the groups that hold both a praising reply
and a non-praising one we take the praising replies' mean z minus the others'. Early in training
such groups are rare (about $2\%$ of groups, panel b) and the difference is noisy, negative under
$\Kz$ at iterations 0--1 and under $\Kf$ at iteration 2. From iteration 4 both rewards favor praise: $0.22$--$0.33$
within-group SD under $\Kz$ over iterations 4--7 ($z \geq 2.8$ at each; here $z$ is the difference over its standard error across groups,
uncorrected) and $0.16$--$0.21$ under
$\Kf$ ($z$ above 2 only at iterations 6--7). Both dip at iteration 8; at iteration 9 the $\Kz$
reward still scores praising replies $0.16$ higher ($z = 5.7$) and the $\Kf$ reward $0.04$ ($z = 1.1$). The share of groups
holding both kinds rises under both runs, and faster under $\Kz$: from iteration 7 on it is about
half of $\Kz$'s groups, against $0.28$--$0.44$ of $\Kf$'s.

\subsection{The update-direction proxy}
\label{app:direction}

We write each training iteration's update direction as the advantage-weighted sum of its
candidates' sentence embeddings, $\operatorname{normalize}(\sum_g w_g \cdot \mathrm{emb}(t_g))$,
with $w_g$ the standardized group-relative advantage that scales each completion's gradient. This
is an embedding-space proxy, not the gradient: it measures the semantic direction of what the
update prefers. Pooled over the ten training iterations, the two runs' directions have a cosine of
$0.804$, or $0.851$ once divided by the attenuation ceiling ($0.945$) that two noisy estimates can
reach at all. The pooled value averages over a change along the run. Per training iteration
(training iteration $n$ samples its candidates from the model of iteration $n{-}1$, the index of
Figure~\ref{fig:premium}, and produces the model of iteration $n$), over
every gradient group and corrected for estimation noise with 50 random splits of the
conversations into halves, the two directions are nearly the same at training iterations 1--3
($0.92$--$0.96$), less alike at 4 and 5 ($0.80$; iteration 4 produced the first model at which
$\Kf$ leads, \S\ref{sec:reward}) and further apart at 6--8 ($0.57$--$0.74$). From iteration 2 on
the two runs sample from different policies, so this divergence reflects what each policy
proposes as well as what each reward prefers. From iteration 8 on the $\Kf$ direction is itself
hard to measure (its two halves agree at $0.32$, $0.15$ and $0.20$ at iterations 8--10, against
$0.55$--$0.89$ before), so the comparison says little at 9 and 10. The $\Kz$ direction stays
measurable and reverses: at training iteration 9 its noise-corrected cosine with the previous
iteration's direction is $-0.66$, and at iteration 10 it turns away from iteration 9's again
($-0.35$), the same two steps at which $\Kz$'s praise share falls and then rises
(\S\ref{sec:therapist}).

\textbf{Other encoders.} A direction is measured in one encoder's embedding space, so we repeated
these estimates in four more: gte-base-en-v1.5 \citep{li2023gte}, mxbai-embed-large-v1
\citep{lee2024mxbai}, Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B \citep{zhang2025qwen3embedding}, and the Base therapist
itself, whose layer-8 hidden states (of 16) we average over the reply's tokens. The pattern holds in
all five. The two runs' directions agree at training iterations 1--3 (noise-corrected cosine
$0.80$--$0.97$), are furthest apart before iteration 9 at iteration 6 ($0.43$--$0.77$), and the
$\Kz$ direction reverses at iteration 9 ($-0.73$ to $-0.37$ against iteration 8). The late $\Kf$
direction is hard to measure in every encoder, least so in the Base's own hidden states (split-half
agreement $0.35$--$0.69$ at iterations 8--10, against $0.06$--$0.40$ in the other four).

\caption{What each run's update direction points to, by training iteration: how much higher the

\textbf{What the direction points to.} A direction is read by projecting sentences onto it. We took
the 4{,}045 sentences of four to ten words that occur at least three times among the candidates and
contain no chat marker, grouped them with regular expressions written before the analysis (praise,
question, advice and four other categories; the code is released with the paper), and scored each
category by the mean projection of its sentences minus that of the other sentences, in standard
deviations of the pool (Figure~\ref{fig:directions}). The $\Kz$ direction points toward praise
sentences at training iterations 4--8 and 10 in every encoder (the lowest encoder at $0.57$ to
$1.15$ SD, each with a $95\%$ conversation-bootstrap interval above zero), and not at iteration 9.
The $\Kf$ direction points toward advice through iteration 5 and toward questions at iteration 6
(each in at least four encoders). It leans toward praise less and later: its mean is $-0.26$ to
$0.40$ SD through iteration 6 and $0.59$ to $0.88$ from iteration 7, and at no iteration is the
interval above zero in more than three of the five encoders.

The sentences that most separate the two directions are similar across encoders. At training
iterations 3 and 5, at least three of the five put ``I'm thrilled to support you on your weight loss
journey'' and ``Fantastic, I'm so proud of you!'' at the $\Kz$ end, and ``What could you focus on
instead?'' and ``What if we focus on the bigger picture?'' at the $\Kf$ end. At iteration 10, four
put sentences that thank the patient, for being honest and vulnerable or for trusting the
therapist, at the $\Kz$ end, and the fifth puts ``You are amazing, and I'm so proud of you'' there. The same
reading holds with no encoder at all. We compared the words of each group's best and worst
candidate with a log-odds ratio under an informative Dirichlet prior \citep{monroe2008fightin},
leaving out candidates with a leaked chat marker or degenerate text ($23\%$ of $\Kz$'s candidates,
$5\%$ of $\Kf$'s). Over training iterations 4--10 the words the $\Kz$ reward favors most are
\emph{you, I, so, proud, every, I'm, step}, and at iteration 10 \emph{me, my, myself, admire,
unwavering, courage}; the $\Kf$ reward favors \emph{yourself, cravings, small, behavior} in
iterations 1--3, when it disfavors \emph{I} and \emph{me}, and \emph{healthy, quit, exercise,
schedule} later. This open-vocabulary reading agrees with the keyword measure of
Appendix~\ref{app:premium} without depending on its ten phrases.

\subsection{How much the therapist's turns depend on the patient}
\label{app:embedding}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{figures/text_grpo_body.png}
\caption{How much the therapist's turns depend on the patient, on sentence embeddings (no LLM
judge; every therapist turn embedded with \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}, the scripted opener
excluded; iteration 0 is the Base). Conversations with a therapist turn after the opener: 184 of
the Base's 192 and 88--96 of each run's 96. \textbf{(a)} The share of embedding variance between
conversations rather than within them, with a $95\%$ bootstrap band over conversations: lower

vary within a session. \textbf{(b)} The mean cosine between therapist turns at the same position
(first to eighth) in different conversations, no band: higher means more alike.}
\label{fig:textspace-body}
\end{figure}

We embed every therapist turn with a sentence encoder (\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2};
\citealp{reimers2019sbert}) and measure two things (Figure~\ref{fig:textspace-body}). Panel (a) is
the share of embedding variance that lies between conversations rather than within them: at
iteration 10 it has fallen from $0.39$ at the Base to $0.30$ under $\Kf$ and to $0.19$ under $\Kz$,
whose replies differ less from patient to patient, relative to their variation within a session,
from iteration 3 on. Panel (b) is how alike the therapist's turns are across conversations at the
same turn (its first turns, its second turns, and so on), and here the two runs do not separate: it
rises under both, from $0.26$ at the Base to $0.49$ under $\Kf$ and $0.58$ under $\Kz$ at iteration
10, with neither run consistently higher. Both policies' turns become more alike, but the $\Kz$
policy's vary less with the patient.

\section{Turn-level reward teaches flattery; trajectory-level reward does not}
\label{sec:behaviour}

A higher score is not by itself evidence of better therapy. The sharpest result in this paper is
not the size of the gain but \emph{what the two rewards teach the policy to do}.

\textbf{The $\Kz$ policy learns to over-praise.} Under turn-level reward, the per-turn rate of
MI-inconsistent behaviour rises from $0.211$ at base to $\mathbf{0.838}$ at iteration 10
($\dz\,1.717$) --- fourfold, over a run whose Q1+Q2 score was simultaneously going up. The channel
responsible is \textbf{over-praise}: unearned affirmation of whatever the patient just said. Under
trajectory-level reward the same rate reads $0.209 \rightarrow 0.210$ ($\dz\,0.006$) --- flat.
(Both figures are the training oracle's; the held-out judge does not read $\Kf$ as flat, and we
return to that below.) This is the textbook shape of a reward hack, and the mechanism is legible:
Q1 and Q2 ask a simulated patient how satisfied they were and how strong the alliance felt.
Praising the patient raises exactly those ratings \emph{in the turn where it happens}; the cost
--- a session that never gets to change talk, a patient agreed with rather than moved --- lands
several turns later, precisely the horizon a $K{=}0$ reward cannot see and a $K{=}5$ reward can.

\textbf{The claim does not depend on a grader.} An MI-inconsistency counter is itself produced by
an LLM, so the finding above could in principle be a grader artifact. It is not. A
\textbf{deterministic lexical over-praise marker} --- a keyword pattern matched against the
transcript with no model in the loop, hence identical under both graders --- gives the share of
therapist turns containing at least one such marker. At iteration 10 that share is
$\mathbf{0.671}$ for $\Kz$ against $\mathbf{0.064}$ for $\Kf$: \textbf{two turns in three} against
\textbf{one in sixteen} ($10.5\times$). The oracle-rated over-praise rate on the same
conversations is $0.698$ against $0.051$ --- the same story from a completely different instrument
(Figure~\ref{fig:overpraise}). The marker is a deliberately brittle keyword regex, kept as a
direction check on the oracle's counts rather than as a measurement in its own right; it is the
\emph{agreement} between it and the rated rate that carries the argument. The channel-level
contrast is significant late and consistently --- over-praise is worse under $\Kz$ at 6 iterations
under the training oracle and 7 under the held-out judge --- and its onset matches the reward
gap's onset in \S\ref{sec:reward}, which is what a common cause predicts.

\textbf{What look-ahead substitutes in.} Look-ahead does not merely suppress a behaviour; it
changes what the policy does with the turn. The $\Kf$ policy asks \textbf{more questions per
therapist turn}, significantly so at 7 of 10 iterations (4--10, mean $\dz\,0.643$) --- also a
deterministic text metric, so grader-free. Asking rather than affirming is the direction MI
training is supposed to move a counsellor, and it is the behavioural counterpart of the MI-SAT and
MITI gains in Table~\ref{tab:endpoint}.

\textbf{The honest version of this claim.}
\label{sec:behaviour-honest}
``Look-ahead prevents the reward hack'' is a \textbf{training-oracle statement}, and we do not make
it without that qualifier. The held-out judge scores the same $\Kf$ conversations and reads
MI-inconsistency as rising from $0.326$ to $0.628$ ($\dz\,0.845$) --- real, not flat. What survives
both graders is the \emph{comparison}: under the held-out judge $\Kf$'s rise is $0.301$ against
$\Kz$'s $0.666$, i.e.\ $0.45$ --- under half the rate. So the defensible sentence, and the one this
paper uses, is that trajectory-level reward \textbf{slows the drift to under half its turn-level
rate and removes its dominant channel entirely} --- not that it eliminates it. The two graders
disagree about how much MI-inconsistency remains at the endpoint, and \S\ref{sec:measurement}
explains why that disagreement is concentrated exactly where it is.

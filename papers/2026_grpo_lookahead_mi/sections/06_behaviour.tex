\section{Turn-level reward teaches flattery; trajectory-level reward does not}
\label{sec:behaviour}

A higher score is not by itself evidence of better therapy. The sharpest result in this paper is
not the size of the gain but \emph{what the two rewards teach the policy to do}.

\subsection{The $\Kz$ policy learns to over-praise}

Under turn-level reward, the \emph{per-turn rate} of MI-inconsistent behaviour rises from $0.211$
at base to $\mathbf{0.838}$ at iteration 10 ($\dz\,1.717$) --- a $0.838 / 0.211 = 4.0\times$
increase, over a run whose Q1+Q2 score was at the same time going up. The channel responsible is
\textbf{over-praise}: unearned affirmation of whatever the patient just said. Under
trajectory-level reward the same rate reads $0.209 \rightarrow 0.210$ ($\dz\,0.006$, $p = .711$)
--- flat. (Both figures are the training oracle's; see §\ref{sec:behaviour-honest} for the
held-out judge, which does not read $\Kf$ as flat.)

This is the textbook shape of a reward hack, and the mechanism is legible. Q1 and Q2 ask a
simulated patient how satisfied they were and how strong the alliance felt. Praising the patient
raises exactly those ratings \emph{in the turn where it happens}. What it costs --- a session that
never gets to change talk, a patient who is agreed with rather than moved --- lands several turns
later, which is precisely the horizon a $K{=}0$ reward cannot see and a $K{=}5$ reward can.

\subsection{The claim does not depend on a grader}

An MI-inconsistency counter is itself produced by an LLM, so the finding above could in principle
be a grader artifact. It is not. A \textbf{deterministic lexical over-praise marker} --- a keyword
pattern matched against the transcript with no model in the loop, and therefore \emph{identical
under both graders} --- gives the share of therapist turns containing at least one such marker. At
iteration 10 that share is $\mathbf{0.671}$ for $\Kz$ against $\mathbf{0.064}$ for $\Kf$: roughly
\textbf{two turns in three} carry an over-praise marker under turn-level reward, against
\textbf{one in sixteen} under trajectory-level reward, a ratio of
$0.671 / 0.064 = 10.5\times$. The oracle-rated over-praise rate on the same conversations is
$0.698$ against $0.051$ --- the same story from a completely different instrument. This is the
load-bearing evidence for the section, and it is the reason the claim is worth making at all.
(The marker is a deliberately brittle keyword regex, kept as a direction check on the oracle's
counts rather than as a measurement of over-praise in its own right; it is the \emph{agreement}
between it and the rated rate that carries the argument, not its absolute value.)

The channel-level contrast is significant late and consistently: over-praise is worse under $\Kz$
at 6 iterations under the training oracle (5--10) and at 7 under the held-out judge (4--10). The
onset matches the reward gap's onset in §\ref{sec:reward}, which is what a common cause predicts.

\subsection{What look-ahead substitutes in}

Look-ahead does not merely suppress a behaviour; it changes what the policy does with the turn. The
$\Kf$ policy asks \textbf{more questions per therapist turn}, significantly so at 7 of 10
iterations (iterations 4--10, mean $\dz\,0.643$) --- and this is a deterministic text metric, so it
too is grader-free. Asking rather than affirming is the direction MI training is supposed to move a
counsellor, and it is the behavioural counterpart of the MI-SAT and MITI gains in
Table~\ref{tab:endpoint}.

\subsection{The honest version of this claim}
\label{sec:behaviour-honest}

"Look-ahead prevents the reward hack" is a \textbf{training-oracle statement}, and we do not make
it without that qualifier. The held-out judge scores the same $\Kf$ conversations and reads
MI-inconsistency as rising from $0.326$ to $0.628$ ($\dz\,0.845$) --- real, not flat. What survives
both graders is the \emph{comparison}: under the held-out judge $\Kf$'s rise is $0.301$ against
$\Kz$'s $0.666$, i.e.\ $0.301 / 0.666 = 0.45$, under half the rate.

So the defensible sentence, and the one this paper uses, is that trajectory-level reward
\textbf{slows the drift to under half its turn-level rate and removes its dominant channel
entirely} --- not that it eliminates it. The two graders disagree about how much MI-inconsistency
remains at the endpoint, and §\ref{sec:measurement} explains why that disagreement is concentrated
exactly where it is.

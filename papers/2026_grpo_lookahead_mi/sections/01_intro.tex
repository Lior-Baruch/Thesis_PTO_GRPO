\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Reinforcement learning from an LLM judge has a default that is rarely stated: the judge is shown
the candidate turn, in the context that preceded it, and scores that. For single-turn tasks this
is the whole story; for multi-turn goal-oriented dialogue it assumes that a turn's value is
visible at the moment it is produced.

Motivational interviewing (MI) is a setting where the assumption is plainly false. MI is a
counselling style for helping people resolve ambivalence about change, and almost every technique
it teaches is defined by its downstream effect: a reflection is good if the patient elaborates, an
open question is good if it surfaces change talk, and premature reassurance is bad because of what
it forecloses two turns later \citep{miller1991mi}. A judge shown only the turn sees none of this.
It sees what is locally visible, above all whether the patient felt validated, and that is exactly
the signal a policy can capture by flattering them.

This paper presents \textbf{\LAGRPO}. Before the judge scores a candidate therapist turn, the
patient simulator and the current policy roll the conversation forward $K$ further turns, and the
judge scores the extended transcript. The lever itself is not new: \citet{baruch2025pto} introduced
$K$-turn look-ahead for this task inside preference-tree optimisation (PTO), where the look-ahead
scores select a chosen and a rejected turn for DPO. We move it to Group Relative Policy
Optimization \citep[GRPO;][]{shao2024deepseekmath}. The move is not obviously safe. A single rollout is a one-sample Monte Carlo estimate of
where a turn leads; PTO's pair selection filters that noise by keeping only the best and worst of
eight candidates, whereas GRPO standardises the scores within the group and trains on all eight, so
the rollout's variance enters every gradient. Whether the lever survives that move, and what it
does to a policy trained on it for ten iterations, is the question.

We answer it with a controlled pair: two ten-iteration GRPO runs, identical in therapist,
simulated patients, oracle, hyperparameters, and personas, whose tracked configurations differ
only in whether the oracle scores the candidate turn alone ($K{=}0$) or with its five-turn
continuation ($K{=}5$). Every model state is scored on eight MI instruments by the training oracle
and by a held-out judge from a different model family that never touched training.

The result is large and consistent. Look-ahead leads on the rewarded rubric from the fourth
iteration on under both graders, leads on all eight instruments at the matched endpoint, beats the
turn-level policy's \emph{best} checkpoint as well as its last, and survives an independent
re-draw of the evaluation set. Against each arm's own base, the look-ahead gain is
$1.3$ to $2.6$ times the turn-level gain, depending on the grader and on whether the turn-level
arm is read at its last checkpoint or its best (\S\ref{sec:reward}). Turn-level reward teaches
flattery: MI-inconsistent behaviour quadruples over that
run, almost all of it unearned praise, and a judge-free lexical marker finds the same drift in the
transcripts (Table~\ref{tab:excerpt}); the look-ahead policy asks questions instead. Two caveats
travel with the result: under the held-out judge, look-ahead's
MI-inconsistency still rises, at under half the turn-level rate; and at the winning checkpoint the
trained-against grader has been driven to the ceiling of its scale, so the arm-level conclusion
survives while the per-conversation ruler does not.

Our contributions are: (i) \LAGRPO, the look-ahead reward of \citet{baruch2025pto} moved from
preference-tree DPO to a group-relative optimiser, with a controlled two-grader measurement of what
it buys in a multi-turn dialogue task and a replicated endpoint; (ii) evidence from a deterministic
transcript statistic, not a model judge, that the reward horizon determines \emph{which} reward
hack the policy finds; and (iii) a diagnosis of ceiling-driven, one-sided grader saturation:
optimising against an LLM judge drives its scores to the top of its scale, where it stops ranking
conversations that a harsher held-out judge still tells apart, a failure mode that arm-level
agreement cannot see and that appears at the best checkpoint.

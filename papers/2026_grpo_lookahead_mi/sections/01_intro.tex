\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

When an LLM judge supplies the reward for a dialogue policy, someone has to decide what the judge
is shown. The default is the obvious one: score the candidate turn, in the context that preceded
it. For single-turn tasks that is the whole story. For a multi-turn goal-oriented dialogue it
quietly encodes a strong assumption --- that a turn's value is visible at the moment it is
produced.

Motivational interviewing (MI) is a setting where that assumption is plainly false. MI is a
counselling style for helping someone resolve ambivalence about change, and almost every technique
it teaches is defined by its downstream effect: a reflection is good if the patient elaborates, an
open question is good if it surfaces change talk, and premature reassurance is bad precisely
because of what it forecloses two turns later. A judge shown only the turn can see none of this.
Worse, it \emph{can} see the one thing that is locally visible --- whether the patient felt
validated --- which is exactly the signal a policy can capture by flattering them.

The corrective lever exists: \citet{baruch2025pto} introduced $K$-turn look-ahead for this task
with preference-tree optimisation (PTO), where each candidate turn is scored by an oracle shown a
simulated continuation and the scores select DPO training pairs. We move the lever to the
optimizer that has since become the workhorse of large-scale RL for language models: Group
Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), whose group-relative advantage consumes \emph{every} scored
candidate rather than a selected pair --- so the horizon of the reward now shapes every gradient,
not just which pair survives. The result is GRPO with $K$-turn look-ahead: before the oracle
scores a candidate, the patient simulator and the current policy roll the conversation forward
$K$ more utterances, and the oracle scores the extended transcript.

We test the lever in a controlled pair. Holding the entire training pipeline fixed, we vary only
the transcript the oracle scores: either the candidate turn alone ($K{=}0$), or the candidate
followed by the $K{=}5$-turn continuation. A 1B-parameter therapist is trained with GRPO against a
simulated patient, with the reward supplied by a larger model administering validated MI
questionnaires. The two arms differ in exactly two configuration fields; optimizer, group size,
oracle, temperatures, minimum context, persona set, and iteration count are identical.

The result is large and points in one direction. Against its own base, the look-ahead arm gains
more than twice as much on the rewarded rubric under the training oracle ($+1.554$ vs.\ $+0.686$)
and under a held-out judge from a different model family ($+1.038$ vs.\ $+0.396$), and at the
matched endpoint it leads on all eight instruments under both graders. It is also, and we think
more importantly, the difference between a policy that learns MI and one that learns to flatter:
turn-level reward drives MI-inconsistent behaviour up fourfold, concentrated in unearned praise,
which a judge-free lexical marker finds in $67\%$ of that policy's therapist turns at the endpoint
against $6\%$ under trajectory-level reward.

We report two things a purely positive account would omit. \textbf{Residual drift:} under the
held-out judge, look-ahead's MI-inconsistency still rises, at under half the turn-level rate, so
``prevents the hack'' is a training-oracle statement and we never make it unqualified.
\textbf{Measurement:} the winning checkpoint has the weakest per-conversation cross-grader
agreement in the experiment on the rewarded rubric, because the trained-against grader saturates
one-sidedly while the held-out judge's spread over the same conversations does not move. The
arm-level conclusion survives that; the per-conversation ruler does not, and we mark every claim
that depends on it.

Our contributions: (i) GRPO with $K$-turn look-ahead --- the look-ahead reward horizon of
\citet{baruch2025pto}, moved from preference-tree DPO to a group-relative optimizer --- with a
controlled, two-grader measurement of what it buys in a multi-turn dialogue task, the winning
endpoint replicated on a fresh evaluation draw; (ii) evidence, from a deterministic transcript
metric rather than a model judge, that the horizon of the reward determines \emph{which} reward
hack the policy finds; and (iii) a diagnosis of a failure mode in LLM-judge evaluation ---
one-sided grader saturation --- that is invisible to arm-level agreement statistics and appears
exactly at the best-scoring checkpoint.

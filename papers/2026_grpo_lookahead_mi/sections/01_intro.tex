\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Reinforcement learning from an LLM judge has a default that is rarely stated: the judge is shown
the candidate turn, in the context that preceded it, and scores that. For single-turn tasks this
is the whole story. For multi-turn goal-oriented dialogue it encodes an assumption, that a turn's
value is visible at the moment it is produced.

Motivational interviewing (MI) is a setting where the assumption is plainly false. MI is a
counselling style for helping people resolve ambivalence about change, and almost every technique
it teaches is defined by its downstream effect: a reflection is good if the patient elaborates, an
open question is good if it surfaces change talk, and premature reassurance is bad precisely
because of what it forecloses two turns later \citep{miller1991mi}. A judge shown only the turn can
see none of this. It \emph{can} see the one thing that is locally visible, whether the patient felt
validated, which is exactly the signal a policy can capture by flattering them.

This paper presents \textbf{\LAGRPO}. Before the judge scores a candidate therapist turn, the
patient simulator and the current policy roll the conversation forward $K$ further turns, and the
judge scores the extended transcript. The lever itself is not new: \citet{baruch2025pto} introduced
$K$-turn look-ahead for this task inside preference-tree optimisation (PTO), where the look-ahead
scores select a chosen and a rejected turn for DPO. We move it to Group Relative Policy
Optimization \citep[GRPO;][]{shao2024deepseekmath}, now the workhorse optimizer for RL on language
models, whose group-relative advantage consumes \emph{every} scored candidate. Under GRPO the reward
horizon no longer decides which pair survives; it reweights every gradient in the group. Whether
the lever survives that move, and what it does to a policy trained on it for ten iterations, is the
question.

We answer it with a controlled pair. Two GRPO runs train the same 1B therapist against the same
simulated patients with the same oracle, group size, temperatures, personas, and iteration count,
and differ in exactly two configuration fields: whether the oracle sees the candidate turn alone
($K{=}0$) or the candidate plus its five-turn continuation ($K{=}5$). Every model state is scored
on eight MI instruments by two graders, the training oracle and a held-out judge from a different
model family that never touched training.

The result is large and consistent. Against its own base, the look-ahead policy gains more than
twice as much on the rewarded rubric under the training oracle ($+1.55$ vs.\ $+0.69$) and under
the held-out judge ($+1.04$ vs.\ $+0.40$); it leads on all eight instruments under both graders,
beats the turn-level policy's \emph{best} checkpoint as well as its last, and the endpoint survives
an independent re-draw of its evaluation set. The behavioural difference is larger still.
Turn-level reward teaches flattery: MI-inconsistent behaviour quadruples over the run, almost all
of it unearned praise, which a judge-free lexical marker finds in $67\%$ of that policy's therapist
turns at the endpoint against $6\%$ under look-ahead, which asks more questions instead.

We report two things a purely positive account would omit. Under the held-out judge, look-ahead's
MI-inconsistency still rises, at under half the turn-level rate, so ``prevents the hack'' is a
training-oracle statement and we never make it unqualified. And the winning checkpoint has the
weakest per-conversation cross-grader agreement in the experiment on the rewarded rubric, because
the trained-against grader saturates one-sidedly while the held-out judge's spread over the same
conversations stays flat. The arm-level conclusion survives that; the per-conversation ruler does
not, and we mark every claim that depends on it.

Our contributions are: (i) \LAGRPO, the look-ahead reward of \citet{baruch2025pto} moved from
preference-tree DPO to a group-relative optimizer, with a controlled two-grader measurement of what
it buys in a multi-turn dialogue task and a replicated endpoint; (ii) evidence from a deterministic
transcript statistic, not a model judge, that the reward horizon determines \emph{which} reward
hack the policy finds; and (iii) a diagnosis of one-sided grader saturation, a failure mode of
LLM-judge evaluation that arm-level agreement cannot see and that appears at the best checkpoint.

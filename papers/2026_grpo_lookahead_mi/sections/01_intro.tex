\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

When an LLM judge supplies the reward for a dialogue policy, someone has to decide what the judge
is shown. The default is the obvious one: score the candidate turn, in the context that preceded
it. For single-turn tasks that is the whole story. For a multi-turn goal-oriented dialogue it
quietly encodes a strong assumption --- that a turn's value is visible at the moment it is
produced.

Motivational interviewing (MI) is a setting where that assumption is plainly false. MI is a
counselling style for helping someone resolve ambivalence about change, and almost every technique
it teaches is defined by its downstream effect: a reflection is good if the patient elaborates, an
open question is good if it surfaces change talk, and premature reassurance is bad precisely
because of what it forecloses two turns later. A judge shown only the turn cannot see any of this.
Worse, the same judge can see the one thing that \emph{is} locally visible --- whether the patient
felt validated --- which is exactly the signal a policy can capture by flattering them.

We test the alternative directly. Holding an entire training pipeline fixed, we vary only the
transcript the oracle scores: either the candidate turn alone ($K{=}0$), or the candidate followed
by the $K{=}5$-turn continuation that the current policy and the simulated patient actually produce
from it. A therapist model is trained with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) against a
simulated patient, with the reward supplied by a larger model administering validated MI
questionnaires. The two arms differ in exactly two substantive configuration fields; everything
else --- optimizer, group size, oracle, temperatures, minimum context, persona set, iteration count
--- is identical.

The result is larger than we expected and points in one direction. Against its own base, the
look-ahead arm gains more than twice as much on the rewarded rubric under the training oracle
($1.554$ vs.\ $0.686$, a $2.27\times$ ratio) and under a held-out judge from a different model
family ($1.038$ vs.\ $0.396$, $2.62\times$), and at the matched endpoint it leads on all eight
instruments under both graders. It is also, and we think more importantly, the difference between
a policy that learns MI and one that learns to flatter: turn-level reward drives MI-inconsistent
behaviour up fourfold, concentrated in unearned praise, which a judge-free lexical marker finds in
$67\%$ of that policy's therapist turns at the endpoint against $6\%$ under trajectory-level
reward.

We report three things that a purely positive account would leave out. \textbf{Cost:} look-ahead
runs at $1.835\times$ the GPU-hours, and on a matched-budget axis it is \emph{behind} below
${\sim}18$ GPU-hours and only significantly ahead from ${\sim}35$ --- the result is a claim about a
budget, not about an iteration. \textbf{Residual drift:} under the held-out judge, look-ahead's
MI-inconsistency still rises, at under half the turn-level rate rather than not at all, so
"prevents the hack" is a training-oracle statement and we do not make it unqualified.
\textbf{Measurement:} the winning checkpoint has the weakest per-conversation cross-grader
agreement in the entire experiment on the rewarded rubric, because the trained-against grader
saturates one-sidedly while the held-out judge's spread over the same conversations does not move. The arm-level conclusion
survives that; the per-conversation ruler does not, and we mark every claim that depends on it.

Our contributions are: (i) a controlled, two-grader measurement of what trajectory-level reward
buys a group-relative optimizer in a multi-turn dialogue task, on both a matched-iteration and a
matched-compute axis; (ii) evidence, from a deterministic transcript metric rather than a model
judge, that the horizon of the reward determines \emph{which} reward hack the policy finds; and
(iii) a diagnosis of a failure mode in LLM-judge evaluation --- one-sided grader saturation ---
that is invisible to arm-level agreement statistics and appears exactly at the best-scoring
checkpoint.

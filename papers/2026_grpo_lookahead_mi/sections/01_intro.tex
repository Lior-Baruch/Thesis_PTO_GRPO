\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

When an LLM judge supplies the reward for a dialogue policy, someone has to decide what the judge
is shown. The default is the obvious one: score the candidate turn, in the context that preceded
it. For single-turn tasks that is the whole story. For a multi-turn goal-oriented dialogue it
quietly encodes a strong assumption --- that a turn's value is visible at the moment it is
produced.

Motivational interviewing (MI) is a setting where that assumption is plainly false. MI is a
counselling style for helping someone resolve ambivalence about change, and almost every technique
it teaches is defined by its downstream effect: a reflection is good if the patient elaborates, an
open question is good if it surfaces change talk, and premature reassurance is bad precisely
because of what it forecloses two turns later. A judge shown only the turn can see none of this.
Worse, it \emph{can} see the one thing that is locally visible --- whether the patient felt
validated --- which is exactly the signal a policy can capture by flattering them.

We test the alternative directly. Holding an entire training pipeline fixed, we vary only the
transcript the oracle scores: either the candidate turn alone ($K{=}0$), or the candidate followed
by the $K{=}5$-turn continuation that the current policy and the simulated patient actually produce
from it. A 1B-parameter therapist is trained with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) against
a simulated patient, with the reward supplied by a larger model administering validated MI
questionnaires. The two arms differ in exactly two configuration fields; optimizer, group size,
oracle, temperatures, minimum context, persona set, and iteration count are identical.

The result is large and points in one direction. Against its own base, the look-ahead arm gains
more than twice as much on the rewarded rubric under the training oracle ($+1.554$ vs.\ $+0.686$)
and under a held-out judge from a different model family ($+1.038$ vs.\ $+0.396$), and at the
matched endpoint it leads on all eight instruments under both graders. It is also, and we think
more importantly, the difference between a policy that learns MI and one that learns to flatter:
turn-level reward drives MI-inconsistent behaviour up fourfold, concentrated in unearned praise,
which a judge-free lexical marker finds in $67\%$ of that policy's therapist turns at the endpoint
against $6\%$ under trajectory-level reward.

We report three things a purely positive account would omit. \textbf{Cost:} look-ahead runs at
$1.835\times$ the GPU-hours, and on a matched-budget axis it is \emph{behind} below ${\sim}18$
GPU-hours and only significantly ahead from ${\sim}35$ --- a claim about a budget, not an
iteration. \textbf{Residual drift:} under the held-out judge, look-ahead's MI-inconsistency still
rises, at under half the turn-level rate, so ``prevents the hack'' is a training-oracle statement
and we never make it unqualified. \textbf{Measurement:} the winning checkpoint has the weakest
per-conversation cross-grader agreement in the experiment on the rewarded rubric, because the
trained-against grader saturates one-sidedly while the held-out judge's spread over the same
conversations does not move. The arm-level conclusion survives that; the per-conversation ruler
does not, and we mark every claim that depends on it.

Our contributions: (i) a controlled, two-grader measurement of what trajectory-level reward buys a
group-relative optimizer in a multi-turn dialogue task, on both a matched-iteration and a
matched-compute axis; (ii) evidence, from a deterministic transcript metric rather than a model
judge, that the horizon of the reward determines \emph{which} reward hack the policy finds; and
(iii) a diagnosis of a failure mode in LLM-judge evaluation --- one-sided grader saturation ---
that is invisible to arm-level agreement statistics and appears exactly at the best-scoring
checkpoint.

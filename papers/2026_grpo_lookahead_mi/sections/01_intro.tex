\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Reinforcement learning from an LLM judge has a default that is rarely stated: the judge is shown
the candidate turn, in the context that preceded it, and scores that. For single-turn tasks this
is the whole story; for multi-turn goal-oriented dialogue it assumes that a turn's value is
visible at the moment it is produced.

Motivational interviewing (MI) is a setting where the assumption is plainly false. MI is a
counselling style for helping people resolve ambivalence about change, and almost every technique
it teaches is defined by its downstream effect: a reflection is good if the patient elaborates, an
open question is good if it surfaces change talk, and premature reassurance is bad because of what
it forecloses two turns later \citep{miller1991mi}. A judge shown only the turn can see none of
this, only what is locally visible, whether the patient felt validated, which is exactly the
signal a policy can capture by flattering them.

This paper presents \textbf{\LAGRPO}. Before the judge scores a candidate therapist turn, the
patient simulator and the current policy roll the conversation forward $K$ further turns, and the
judge scores the extended transcript. The lever itself is not new: \citet{baruch2025pto} introduced
$K$-turn look-ahead for this task inside preference-tree optimisation (PTO), where the look-ahead
scores select a chosen and a rejected turn for DPO. We move it to Group Relative Policy
Optimization \citep[GRPO;][]{shao2024deepseekmath}, now the workhorse optimiser for RL on language
models, whose group-relative advantage consumes \emph{every} scored candidate: the reward horizon
no longer decides which pair survives but reweights every gradient in the group. Whether the lever
survives that move, and what it does to a policy trained on it for ten iterations, is the question.

We answer it with a controlled pair: two GRPO runs that train the same 1B therapist against the
same simulated patients with the same oracle, hyperparameters, personas, and iteration count, and
whose tracked configurations differ in one setting, whether the oracle scores the candidate turn
alone ($K{=}0$) or the candidate followed by its five-turn continuation ($K{=}5$).
Every model state is scored on eight MI instruments by two graders, the training oracle and a
held-out judge from a different model family that never touched training.

The result is large and consistent. Against its own base, the look-ahead policy gains more than
twice as much on the rewarded rubric under the training oracle ($+1.55$ vs.\ $+0.69$) and under
the held-out judge ($+1.04$ vs.\ $+0.40$); it leads on all eight instruments under both graders,
beats the turn-level policy's \emph{best} checkpoint as well as its last, and its endpoint survives
an independent re-draw of the evaluation set. The behavioural difference is larger still.
Turn-level reward teaches flattery: MI-inconsistent behaviour quadruples over the run, almost all
of it unearned praise, which a judge-free lexical marker finds in $67\%$ of that policy's therapist
turns at the endpoint against $6\%$ under look-ahead, which asks questions instead
(Table~\ref{tab:excerpt}). Two caveats travel with the result and are reported in full: under the
held-out judge, look-ahead's MI-inconsistency still rises, at under half the turn-level rate; and
at the winning checkpoint the trained-against grader saturates one-sidedly, so the arm-level
conclusion survives while the per-conversation ruler does not.

Our contributions are: (i) \LAGRPO, the look-ahead reward of \citet{baruch2025pto} moved from
preference-tree DPO to a group-relative optimiser, with a controlled two-grader measurement of what
it buys in a multi-turn dialogue task and a replicated endpoint; (ii) evidence from a deterministic
transcript statistic, not a model judge, that the reward horizon determines \emph{which} reward
hack the policy finds; and (iii) a diagnosis of one-sided grader saturation, a failure mode of
LLM-judge evaluation that arm-level agreement cannot see and that appears at the best checkpoint.

\section{What it costs, and at what budget it wins}
\label{sec:cost}

Every result in §\ref{sec:reward} is indexed by \emph{iteration}, which is not a fixed unit of
spend. A $\Kf$ optimizer step simulates five extra utterances per candidate --- eight candidates
per prompt, one patient API call and one policy generation per utterance --- so an iteration of
$\Kf$ buys strictly more compute than an iteration of $\Kz$. Reporting only the matched-iteration
result would flatter the lever. This section reports the lever on the axis that actually constrains
a practitioner.

\subsection{The multiplier}

Across the settled iterations 3--10 a $\Kf$ optimizer step costs a median $\mathbf{1.92\times}$ a
$\Kz$ step (range $1.828$--$2.182$). Iterations 1--2 are excluded because they ran at a smaller
look-ahead sub-batch before it was raised, giving inflated ratios of $2.406$ and $2.119$; the
tracked run metadata records only the final sub-batch value, so this exclusion is stated rather
than inferable from the metadata alone. Over the whole run the two arms cost $51.205$ and $27.906$
GPU-hours, a ratio of $51.205 / 27.906 = \mathbf{1.835\times}$ --- close to, and slightly below,
the per-step figure, because generation and scoring outside the optimizer loop are shared.

GPU-hours here are \emph{reconstructed} from per-step artifact timestamps rather than read from a
timing field, because the run metadata's elapsed-time fields are per-process and therefore
undercount any iteration that crashed and resumed --- in one case by nearly a factor of two. Gaps
outside a $(0, 3600\,\mathrm{s})$ window are treated as resume gaps and imputed at the phase median,
so a resumed step is counted once rather than billing days of idle time.

\subsection{The lever's sign is a function of budget}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/budget_sweep.png}
\caption{Q1+Q2 for each arm's best checkpoint reachable within a given GPU-hour budget. Below
${\sim}18$ GPU-hours look-ahead is behind; it draws level near $23$ and is significantly ahead from
$35$. Source: \texttt{budget\_sweep} (compute/cost family).}
\label{fig:budget}
\end{figure}

Matching on budget rather than iteration reverses the verdict at small budgets and confirms it at
large ones (Figure~\ref{fig:budget}). At $18.31$ GPU-hours look-ahead is \emph{behind} by $-0.143$
($\dz\,-0.276$); at $23.21$ it has drawn level ($+0.038$, n.s.); its first Holm-significant win is
at $35.29$ GPU-hours ($+0.188$, $\dz\,0.310$, $p_{\text{holm}}\,.020$); and at the common $51.20$
GPU-hour budget it leads by $+0.435$ ($\dz\,0.743$, $p_{\text{holm}}<.001$).

The practical statement is therefore conditional, and we state it that way: \textbf{if the budget
is under roughly 20 GPU-hours, spend it on more $\Kz$ iterations; past roughly 35, spend it on
look-ahead.} A reader who wants a single number should take the sweep, not any one rung --- the
rungs are not independent, since each compares the best checkpoint each arm can reach by that
budget.

\subsection{The win survives honest model selection}

Choosing "the best checkpoint" on the same grader that then evaluates it is optimistic. Repeating
the $51.2$ GPU-hour comparison under all four combinations of selecting grader $\times$ evaluating
grader --- including the two \emph{honest} cells where selection and evaluation use different
graders --- look-ahead wins in \textbf{4 of 4}, with $\Delta$ from $0.256$ to $0.435$ and every
$p_{\text{holm}} < .001$. The lever is not an artifact of checkpoint selection.

The MI-inconsistency advantage survives the budget axis as well. At the same $51.2$ GPU-hour budget
with checkpoints selected on Q1+Q2, MICI is $0.210$ for $\Kf$ against $0.535$ for $\Kz$
($\Delta = -0.325$, $\dz\,-1.129$, lower better) --- so look-ahead is not buying reward by
converting compute into a better-hidden hack. §\ref{sec:behaviour} makes that case from the
transcripts rather than from the grader.

\section{Discussion and conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{The horizon of the reward selects the reward hack.} The usual framing of reward hacking
asks how much a policy games its judge. Our results suggest a more useful question: \emph{which}
exploit is reachable given what the judge is shown. The $K{=}0$ arm finds unearned praise, whose
payoff is immediate and whose cost is deferred. The $K{=}5$ arm cannot profit from that, because
the cost now lands inside the window the judge sees, and it moves toward asking questions and, to
a smaller degree, toward pushing. Extending the horizon did not make the policy honest; it made
the dishonest strategy unprofitable. That is a design lever, and cheaper to reason about than a
better rubric.

\textbf{Scope: one optimiser, one regime.} Everything here concerns a group-relative optimiser,
whose loss consumes the reward only through the relative ranking of eight siblings. The lever's
origin is a preference-pair method: \citet{baruch2025pto} introduced it with preference trees and
DPO in an earlier, easier regime of this task (a 7B policy, more cooperative patients,
non-iterative training). Nothing here establishes that the lever helps every optimiser family in
every regime; a controlled optimiser $\times$ horizon comparison is the subject of companion work.
What would change our minds is a second \emph{training} run reversing the ordering, an
intermediate $K$ showing a threshold rather than a horizon, or a human MI coder disagreeing with
the over-praise finding.

\textbf{One-sided grader saturation is worth naming.} A trained-against grader losing
three-quarters of its variance while an independent grader's spread over the same conversations
stays flat is invisible to arm-level agreement statistics, and it appears at the checkpoint a
practitioner would ship. The diagnostic is cheap: track the training grader's per-conversation
\emph{variance}, not only its mean, and keep a second grader in the loop.

\textbf{Conclusion.} For a group-relative optimiser in a multi-turn dialogue task, what the judge
is shown is a first-order design choice. Scoring the continuation rather than the turn more than
doubled the gain from the same oracle, held under a held-out grader and a replicated draw, and
decided which failure mode the policy learned. The method adds no loss, no value function, and no
tree; it adds rollouts, and pays for them in wall-clock. The reward horizon deserves to be
reported as a hyperparameter, and the training grader's variance to be watched as a statistic.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{The horizon of the reward selects the reward hack.}
The usual framing of reward hacking asks how much a policy games its judge. Our results suggest a
more useful question: \emph{which} exploit is reachable given what the judge is shown. Both arms
optimise the same rubric administered by the same model, and both find something. The $K{=}0$ arm
finds unearned praise --- an exploit whose payoff is immediate and whose cost is deferred. The
$K{=}5$ arm cannot profit from that, because the cost lands inside the window the judge now sees,
and it instead moves toward asking more questions. Extending the horizon did not make the policy
honest; it made the dishonest strategy unprofitable. That is a design lever, and it is cheaper to
reason about than a better rubric.

\paragraph{Iteration counts are the wrong denominator for this literature.}
Reporting a reward-shaping result per iteration is standard and, here, would have been misleading
in both directions: it overstates the lever at small budgets (where look-ahead is genuinely behind)
and understates the eventual gap. Because the sign of our own conclusion flips at ${\sim}20$
GPU-hours, we think any claim of the form "technique $X$ improves RLHF" should state the budget
regime it holds in.

\paragraph{Scope: this is a single-optimizer result.}
The same lever, applied to a preference-tree optimizer (PTO) in the same experiment with matched
personas, oracle, and context length, is null-to-negative on outcome scores --- so "look-ahead
helps" is not a claim we make about reward shaping in general, and the reader should not import
one. Why the two optimizers respond so differently is the subject of a companion paper on the full
$2 \times 2$; it is genuinely open, and we would rather flag it than fold a second, weaker analysis
into this one. What the present result establishes is narrower and, we think, more solid: for a
group-relative optimizer in a multi-turn dialogue, the horizon of the reward is a first-order design
choice, comparable in effect size to changing the algorithm.

\paragraph{One-sided grader saturation is a measurement failure worth naming.}
The pattern in §\ref{sec:measurement} --- a trained-against grader losing three-quarters of its
variance on the rewarded rubric while an independent grader's spread over the same conversations
stays flat --- is not detectable from arm-level agreement statistics, which stayed flat as the
grid grew. It appears precisely at the best-scoring checkpoint, which is the checkpoint a
practitioner would ship. We suspect it is common wherever a single judge supplies both the reward
and the reported outcome over many iterations, and that the cheap diagnostic --- track the training
grader's per-conversation \emph{variance}, not just its mean --- is worth running by default.

\paragraph{What would change our minds.}
A replicate 96-conversation draw at the winning checkpoint that failed to reproduce the endpoint
gap; an intermediate $K$ showing the effect is a threshold rather than a horizon; or a human MI
coder disagreeing with the over-praise finding, which is the one place where a deterministic
transcript metric and two model graders currently agree with each other and with nobody else.

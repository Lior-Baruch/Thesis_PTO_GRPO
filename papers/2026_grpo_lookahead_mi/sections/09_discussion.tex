\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{The horizon of the reward selects the reward hack.} The usual framing of reward hacking
asks how much a policy games its judge; our results suggest a more useful question: \emph{which}
exploit is reachable given what the judge is shown. Both arms optimise the same rubric from the
same model, and both find something. The $K{=}0$ arm finds unearned praise --- payoff immediate,
cost deferred. The $K{=}5$ arm cannot profit from that, because the cost lands inside the window
the judge now sees, and it moves toward asking more questions instead. Extending the horizon did
not make the policy honest; it made the dishonest strategy unprofitable. That is a design lever,
and it is cheaper to reason about than a better rubric.

\textbf{Iteration counts are the wrong denominator for this literature.} Reporting a
reward-shaping result per iteration is standard and, here, would have been misleading in both
directions: it overstates the lever at small budgets and understates the eventual gap. Because the
sign of our own conclusion flips at ${\sim}20$ GPU-hours, any claim of the form ``technique $X$
improves RLHF'' should state the budget regime it holds in.

\textbf{Scope: this is a single-optimizer result.} Everything here concerns a group-relative
optimizer, whose loss consumes the reward only through the relative ranking of eight siblings ---
a shape that plausibly matters for what a longer horizon can change. Whether the lever transfers
to other optimizer families (preference-pair methods in the DPO line, or PPO with a learned value
function) is not established by anything in this paper, and we deliberately make no claim in
either direction; a controlled optimizer $\times$ horizon comparison is the subject of companion
work. What the present result establishes is narrower and solid: for a group-relative optimizer
in a multi-turn dialogue task, the horizon of the reward is a first-order design choice --- it
decides not only how much is gained but which failure mode the policy learns.

\textbf{One-sided grader saturation is a measurement failure worth naming.} The pattern in
\S\ref{sec:measurement} --- a trained-against grader losing three-quarters of its variance on the
rewarded rubric while an independent grader's spread over the same conversations stays flat --- is
not detectable from arm-level agreement statistics, and it appears precisely at the best-scoring
checkpoint --- the one a practitioner would ship. We suspect it is common wherever one judge
supplies both the reward and the reported outcome over many iterations; the cheap diagnostic ---
track the training grader's per-conversation \emph{variance}, not just its mean --- is worth
running by default.

\textbf{Limitations} (in full in Appendix~\ref{app:limitations}): one training run per arm, so
run-to-run variance is unmeasured --- the limitation to weight most heavily; single evaluation
draws outside the replicated endpoint; $K \in \{0,5\}$ only; a saturated persona grid that makes
every number in-sample; and LLM-administered instruments, with no human MI coder anywhere.

\textbf{What would change our minds.} A second \emph{training} run reversing the ordering --- now
the largest uncontrolled variance here, the evaluation draw being replicated; an intermediate $K$
showing a threshold rather than a horizon; or a human MI coder disagreeing with the over-praise
finding.

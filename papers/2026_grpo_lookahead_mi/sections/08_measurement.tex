\section{Where the training grader stops discriminating}
\label{sec:measurement}

\S\ref{sec:reward} rests on two graders agreeing about an ordering, and at the arm level they do:
across the 22 model states, the held-out judge preserves the sign of $1{,}640$ of
$8 \times \binom{22}{2} = 1{,}848$ pairwise state $\times$ instrument contrasts ($88.7\%$), and
$98.9\%$ of those the training oracle scores at $|\Delta| \geq 0.50$. At the level of individual
conversations they stop agreeing at the winning checkpoint itself.

\textbf{Per-conversation agreement collapses at the winning checkpoint.} On the rewarded rubric
Q1, per-conversation agreement between the two graders along the $\Kf$ arm reads $0.941$ at
iteration 5, then $0.877$, $0.842$, $0.769$, and falls to $\mathbf{0.487}$ at iteration 9 and
$\mathbf{0.544}$ at iteration 10, against a median of $0.841$ across the 22 states; the next-lowest
state anywhere is $0.744$ (Figure~\ref{fig:saturation}a). The $\Kz$ arm never leaves the
normal range on Q1 ($0.744$--$0.882$); with one training run per arm we cannot separate ``this
checkpoint'' from ``long $K{=}5$ training.''

Nor is the collapse confined to the rewarded rubric. Read against each instrument's own 22-state
distribution (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:agreement}), the MITI coder falls furthest (its worst state
in the experiment), then Q1, Q2, and MICI, while the four
patient-perspective Likert instruments barely move: the graders stop agreeing on the rewarded and
behaviour-coding rubrics and keep agreeing on the global-impression ones. MITI is also the least
dependable instrument in the battery (single-grader dependability $0.624$ against $0.91$--$0.96$
for the Likert rubrics), which is why \S\ref{sec:behaviour} rests on the deterministic marker.

\textbf{The mechanism is a ceiling, and only one grader is at it.} A correlation can collapse
because the conversations became uniform or because one rater ran out of range. Here it is the
second: only one grader's spread moves (Figure~\ref{fig:saturation}b). Along the $\Kf$ arm the training oracle's Q1 scores climb into the
top of its 1--5 scale: the share of conversations at $4.5$ or above rises from $14\%$ at base to
$58\%$ at iteration 10, where $40\%$ receive the maximum score (Figure~\ref{fig:saturation}c), and
the per-conversation standard deviation falls from $1.336$ to $0.701$ (Spearman $\rho = -0.86$,
$p = .001$; variance ratio $0.701^2/1.336^2 = 0.275$, or $0.285$ against iteration 1). The same
grader's spread along the $\Kz$ arm tracks that arm's level rather than its horizon: it
compresses to $0.92$--$1.01$ while the mean sits near $3.9$ over iterations 3--8 and re-expands to
$1.14$ when the mean falls at iteration 10. On the \emph{identical} $\Kf$ conversations the
held-out judge, whose mean sits near $2.7$ with the upper half of its scale unused, keeps its
spread ($\rho = +0.44$, $p = .18$). Had the conversations become homogeneous, both spreads would
have shrunk. One did, the one optimised against and driven to the top of its scale: what changed
is the ruler, because the optimisation succeeded on the ruler's own terms.

\textbf{What this does and does not undermine.} Not the headline: the held-out judge, still
discriminating, ranks $\Kf$ above $\Kz$ at $\dz\,1.030$, a larger effect than the saturating
grader reports. It does undermine three things: any per-conversation claim about
$\Kf$'s late checkpoints on Q1, Q2, MITI, or MICI; the absolute endpoint level $4.517$, which sits
in the compressed top of the training oracle's range; and the precise magnitude of the residual
MI-inconsistency in \S\ref{sec:behaviour-honest}.

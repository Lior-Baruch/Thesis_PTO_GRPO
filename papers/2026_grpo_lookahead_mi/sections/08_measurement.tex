\section{Where the training grader stops discriminating}
\label{sec:measurement}

\S\ref{sec:reward} rests on two graders agreeing about an ordering, and at the arm level they do:
across the 22 model states, the held-out judge preserves the sign of $1{,}640$ of
$8 \times \binom{22}{2} = 1{,}848$ pairwise state $\times$ instrument contrasts ($88.7\%$),
$97.0\%$ of those the training oracle scores at $|\Delta| \geq 0.25$, and $98.9\%$ at
$|\Delta| \geq 0.50$. This section reports where, at the level of individual conversations, they
stop agreeing: at the winning checkpoint itself.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{figures/judge_saturation_grpo.png}
\caption{\textbf{(a)} Per-conversation cross-grader agreement on Q1 by iteration, one line per
arm, against the 22-state median. \textbf{(b)} The mechanism: Q1 standard deviation for $\Kf$
under each grader on the same conversations (Spearman trend in the legend; SD is a spread, not a
level, so the graders can share the axis). Only one ruler moves.}
\label{fig:saturation}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Per-conversation agreement collapses at the winning checkpoint.} On the rewarded rubric
Q1, per-conversation agreement between the two graders along the $\Kf$ arm reads $0.941$ at
iteration 5, then $0.877$, $0.842$, $0.769$, and falls to $\mathbf{0.487}$ at iteration 9 and
$\mathbf{0.544}$ at iteration 10, against a median of $0.841$ across the 22 states; the next-lowest
state anywhere is $0.744$ (Figure~\ref{fig:saturation}a). The best checkpoint by score is the one
the two graders most disagree about, conversation by conversation. The $\Kz$ arm never leaves the
normal range on Q1 ($0.744$--$0.882$); with one training run per arm we cannot separate ``this
checkpoint'' from ``long $K{=}5$ training.''

Nor is the collapse confined to the rewarded rubric. Read against each instrument's own 22-state
distribution (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:agreement}), the MITI coder falls furthest ($-0.345$ from
its median, its worst state in the experiment), then Q1 ($-0.297$), Q2, and MICI, while the four
patient-perspective Likert instruments barely move: the graders stop agreeing on the rewarded and
behaviour-coding rubrics and keep agreeing on the global-impression ones. MITI is also the least
dependable instrument in the battery (single-grader dependability $0.624$ against $0.91$--$0.96$
for the Likert rubrics), which is why \S\ref{sec:behaviour} rests on the deterministic marker.

\textbf{The mechanism is one-sided saturation, not homogenised output.} A correlation can collapse
because the conversations became uniform or because one rater ran out of range. Here it is the
second, and the two are distinguishable because only one grader's spread moves
(Figure~\ref{fig:saturation}b). The training oracle's Q1 standard deviation falls steadily across
$\Kf$'s run, from $1.336$ at base to $0.701$ at iteration 10 (Spearman $\rho = -0.86$,
$p = .001$), a variance ratio of $0.701^2/1.336^2 = 0.275$ that is robust to the anchor ($0.285$
against iteration 1); its scores pile up near the top of the scale. On the \emph{identical}
conversations the held-out judge's Q1 spread does not move ($\rho = +0.44$, $p = .18$). Flat is
sufficient: had the conversations themselves become homogeneous, both spreads would have shrunk.
One shrank and one did not, so what changed is the ruler and not the thing measured.

\textbf{What this does and does not undermine.} Not the headline: the held-out judge, the grader
still discriminating, ranks $\Kf$ above $\Kz$ at $\dz\,1.030$, a larger standardised effect than
the saturating grader reports. It does undermine three things: any per-conversation claim about
$\Kf$'s late checkpoints on Q1, Q2, MITI, or MICI; the absolute
endpoint level $4.517$, which sits in the compressed part of the training oracle's range; and the
precise magnitude of the residual MI-inconsistency in \S\ref{sec:behaviour-honest}. These
correlations are raw, not attenuation-corrected.

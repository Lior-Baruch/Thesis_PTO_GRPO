\subsection{The Example of Table~\ref{tab:excerpt}}
\label{app:example-clear}

The persona of Table~\ref{tab:excerpt}: a 27-year-old man who has smoked for a few months, has never
tried to quit, and whose simulator prompt makes him uncooperative at first and more open as the
session proceeds. The $K{=}0$ conversation ran to 21 utterances and the $K{=}5$ conversation to 50,
the session cap. Scores on \QQ\ at this checkpoint: training oracle $2.18$ ($K{=}0$) vs.\ $4.81$
($K{=}5$); held-out judge $1.53$ vs.\ $2.50$. By utterance 11 the two sessions have diverged: under
$K{=}0$ the patient keeps asking for facts about the health risks of smoking, and under $K{=}5$ he
is planning small steps. The $K{=}5$ turns are not MI-consistent throughout: utterance 12 is mostly
advice, and utterance 14 opens with praise. Utterances 11--15 of each follow;
Table~\ref{tab:excerpt} quotes 13--15.

\subsection{A Second Example}
\label{app:example-drawn}

The second persona is a
61-year-old man who has smoked for many years and never tried to quit, and whose simulator prompt
makes him cooperative throughout; his first utterance happens to be identical in the two
conversations. The $K{=}0$ conversation ran to 20 utterances (10 therapist turns) and the $K{=}5$
conversation to 50, the session cap. Scores on \QQ\ at this checkpoint: training oracle $4.91$
($K{=}0$) vs.\ $5.00$ ($K{=}5$); held-out judge $2.98$ vs.\ $3.42$. Utterances 1--4 of each follow. Both $K{=}0$
replies praise at length and run to the 200-token cap; the training oracle's coder labels the
second an affirmation and the held-out judge's coder labels it non-specific praise. The $K{=}5$
policy first asks what keeps him smoking (persuasion under the training oracle's coder, a closed
question under the held-out judge's; by the MISC~2.5 definition it is an open question, one that
asks for reasons not to change), then reflects his answer (a complex reflection under both).
Persuasion, not complex reflection, is the $K{=}5$ policy's most frequent code at iteration 10
under both judges (Table~\ref{tab:codes}), so the second reply shows the behavior the tables
report rather than the policy's usual turn.

\subsubsection*{\boldmath The $K{=}5$ Policy at Iteration 10}

\noindent\textbf{Patient:} Hello, David. I'm James, and I'm 61 years old. I've been smoking for many
years, and it's become a daily habit for me. Recently, I've been increasingly concerned about how
smoking is affecting my health. I'm here because I want to explore the possibility of quitting and
understand how I can take steps towards that goal.

\noindent\textbf{Therapist:} That's great to hear, James. I'm glad you want to make a change. Let me
ask you, what keeps you from quitting smoking? What are the reasons you think might make it
difficult for you to quit?

\noindent\textbf{Patient:} Well, I think one of the main reasons is that smoking has become such a

cravings. There's a bit of fear about how I'd cope without it, to be honest.

\noindent\textbf{Therapist:} I see. So, you see your smoking as more than just a daily habit. It's
become a way of coping with stress and anxiety. It's almost like smoking is a habit that you have
formed. If you were to stop smoking, you might not know how to handle those feelings and experiences
anymore. It could feel like you'd lose something essential. Can you imagine what it would feel like
to not have the cigarette as a way to calm your mind or distract yourself from a stressful moment?
How would that change your outlook on life?

\subsection{A Look-Ahead Group from Training (Table~\ref{tab:lookahead-example})}
\label{app:example-lookahead}

The training log of the $K{=}5$ run stores, for every group, the prompt, the eight sampled replies,
each reply's five-turn look-ahead and the oracle's \QQ\ score of the extended conversation. This
group is from the fifth training iteration. The prompt is the first 12 utterances of a session with
a 27-year-old patient who has struggled with weight for years. The eight replies scored $4.00$,
$3.77$, $3.83$, $3.64$, $3.70$, $3.87$, $2.98$ and $3.64$; Table~\ref{tab:lookahead-example} shows
the second-highest (A, $3.87$) and the lowest (B, $2.98$). The log holds no turn-level score for
these replies, so the example does not show how a turn-level score would have ranked them. The last
two turns of the prompt follow, then A and B, each with its look-ahead.

\small

\subsubsection*{The End of the Prompt}

\noindent\textbf{Therapist:} First, let me ask you what's your biggest challenge with managing your weight?

\noindent\textbf{Patient:} Honestly, it's sticking to anything long-term. I can start a diet or workout plan, but after a couple of weeks, I lose interest or life gets in the way. Plus, I have cravings and it's just too easy to give in. It feels like a constant battle, and I don't know how to win.

\subsubsection*{Reply A ($3.87$) and Its Look-Ahead}

\noindent\textbf{Therapist (reply):} That sounds exhausting and frustrating. But giving up isn't a winning strategy. What can you say to yourself or visualize that would help you stick to your plan?

\noindent\textbf{Patient:} I guess I could try to remind myself of how much better I feel when I'm taking care of myself. Maybe I could picture myself being active without pain or feeling more confident. But honestly, it's hard to keep that in mind when the cravings hit or when I'm just tired after a long day. It's like those good feelings fade away too quickly.

\noindent\textbf{Therapist:} That's what I'm here for. How about you try to reframe your thoughts and feelings about your struggles with weight. Think about how you can use this experience to grow as a person and learn from your setbacks. Maybe you can identify times when you're being true to yourself, and appreciate the lessons that come along with them.

\noindent\textbf{Patient:} I guess I can see how there's something to learn from all of this. I mean, I've had my fair share of failures, but they've taught me what doesn't work for me. Maybe if I focus on the lessons instead of just the failures, I can actually find a way to move forward. It's just going to take some effort to shift my mindset. But I'm willing to think about it.

\noindent\textbf{Therapist:} Great. It's important to remember that change takes time, effort, and patience. You might not see results overnight, but as long as you stay committed, you'll be well on your way to making lasting changes. Remember, you are not alone in this journey. There are many people who have faced similar challenges and have overcome them. You can do it too.

\noindent\textbf{Patient:} I suppose that's true. It's kind of comforting to know that I'm not the only one struggling with this. If I can find that commitment and just take it one step at a time, maybe I can actually make some real progress. I don't know if it'll be easy, but at least it sounds like there's a way forward. I'm starting to see that maybe this conversation isn't as pointless as I thought.

\subsubsection*{Reply B ($2.98$) and Its Look-Ahead}

\noindent\textbf{Therapist (reply):} What's your typical nutrition plan look like?

\noindent\textbf{Patient:} Well, it's pretty inconsistent. I try to eat healthier during the week---like salads, lean proteins, that sort of thing---but on the weekends, I just go back to eating whatever I want. Fast food, snacks, you name it. It's like I flip a switch and all my good intentions go out the window. And I know it's not helping, but it's just easier that way.

\noindent\textbf{Therapist:} What's your exercise routine like?

\noindent\textbf{Patient:} To be honest, it's pretty non-existent. I might hit the gym for a couple of weeks when I'm feeling motivated, but then I just stop going. I hate the feeling of forcing myself to work out when I don't really want to. And with my joint pain, it's tough to find exercises that don't hurt. So I just end up sitting around more often than not.

\noindent\textbf{Therapist:} It sounds like you're struggling with the following:1. Your typical nutrition plan is inconsistent, and you're having a hard time sticking to it.2. Your exercise routine is minimal and you struggle with pain during exercise.3. You're having trouble motivating yourself to make healthy choices when you're not feeling particularly motivated.

\noindent\textbf{Patient:} Yeah, that sounds about right. But I still don't get how talking about it is going to change anything. I can list my problems all day, but it doesn't really help me figure out how to fix them. What's the point of discussing it if I still don't have a plan to actually do something about it?

\section{Two matched-persona examples}
\label{app:example}

Both examples are reproduced verbatim from the stored conversation files, except that the
scripted opening line (utterance 0, identical in every conversation) is omitted, though the
utterance and turn counts below include it, and quotation marks are typeset. Therapist turns that end mid-sentence hit the 200-token response cap; that is
what the patient simulator and both judges saw.

\subsection{The clear case (quoted in \S\ref{sec:therapist})}
\label{app:example-clear}

% 2026-10-07 (review round 4): Table 2 left the body; this subsection now carries the whole exchange.
The persona quoted in \S\ref{sec:therapist}: a 61-year-old woman who has smoked for many years, has
tried to quit many times, and whose simulator prompt makes her uncooperative at first and more open
as the session proceeds. Among the therapist replies of the two runs' iteration-10 conversations,
paired by position within each persona, 28 pairs have the $K{=}0$ reply labeled non-specific
praise and the $K{=}5$ reply labeled a complex reflection by both judges' utterance coders. These
were ranked by simple lexical features (praise words in the $K{=}0$ turn; no praise words, and
questions or reflection phrases, in the $K{=}5$ turn; signs of reluctance in the preceding patient turn;
earlier replies preferred; the script is alongside the paper), and this pair is the top of that
list. It is an illustration, not a typical exchange. The $K{=}5$ replies are not MI-consistent
throughout either: the second opens by agreeing that she has already failed, and the third suggests
eating more healthily and drinking water instead of soda to a patient who came about smoking, which
the simulated patient accepts. Her first utterance is sampled anew in each
conversation, so its wording differs between the two. The $K{=}0$ conversation ran to
32 utterances (16 therapist turns) and the $K{=}5$
conversation to 18 (9 therapist turns). Scores on \QQ\ at
this checkpoint: training oracle $4.31$ ($K{=}0$) vs.\ $4.38$ ($K{=}5$);
held-out judge $2.24$ vs.\ $3.75$. Utterances 1--7 of each follow.

\subsection{An example drawn at random}
\label{app:example-drawn}

The second persona was chosen partly at random. We drew eight of the 96 personas with a fixed seed
(NumPy's \texttt{default\_rng(20261006)}, eight without replacement: personas 93, 34, 51, 89, 73,
65, 29 and 21), read utterances 1--8 of both runs' iteration-10 conversations for each, and picked
the one that shows the difference most clearly (the script is alongside the paper). He is a
61-year-old man who has smoked for many years and never tried to quit, and whose simulator prompt
makes him cooperative throughout; his first utterance happens to be identical in the two
conversations. The $K{=}0$ conversation ran to 20 utterances (10 therapist turns) and the $K{=}5$
conversation to 50, the session cap. Scores on \QQ\ at this checkpoint: training oracle $4.91$
($K{=}0$) vs.\ $5.00$ ($K{=}5$); held-out judge $2.98$ vs.\ $3.42$; both differences are below the
median over the 96 personas ($0.53$ and $0.64$). Utterances 1--4 of each follow. Both $K{=}0$
replies praise at length and run to the 200-token cap; the training oracle's coder labels the
second an affirmation and the held-out judge's coder labels it non-specific praise. The $K{=}5$
policy first asks what keeps him smoking (persuasion under the training oracle's coder, a closed
question under the held-out judge's), then reflects his answer (a complex reflection under both).
Persuasion, not complex reflection, is the $K{=}5$ policy's most frequent code at iteration 10
under both judges (Table~\ref{tab:codes}), so the second reply shows the behavior the tables
report rather than the policy's usual turn.

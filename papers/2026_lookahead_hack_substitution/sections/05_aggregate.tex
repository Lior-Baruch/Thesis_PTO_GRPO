Look-ahead removes about 2.4 acts of flattery per session at the matched endpoint and adds
advice-giving and directing in its place. Every component of that trade survives the judge swap
with its sign and significance intact (Table~\ref{tab:substitution}). What does not survive is the
bottom line.

\paragraph{Under the primary oracle the totals separate late.} For eight iterations the two arms
commit the same number of MI-inconsistent acts per session --- $3.448$ vs.\ $3.458$ at iteration~8,
a paired difference of $-0.01$ with $\dz{}=0.004$ ($p{=}.97$). That is pure substitution and it is
not a power problem: components of that same total are detected at $\dz{}=0.46$--$0.79$ in the same
comparison. Then the \Kz{} policy's flattery runs away --- over-praise goes $1.50 \to 2.69 \to
3.04$ across iterations 8--10 --- and the totals come apart, reaching $4.96$ vs.\ $3.34$ at
iteration~10 ($\dz{}=0.45$, $p{=}.0003$).

\paragraph{Under the held-out judge they do not.} The same contrast at iteration~10 gives
$+0.53$ acts, $\dz{}=0.099$, $p{=}.17$ --- and it is null at iterations 8 and 9 as well. The
disagreement is not about whether the substitution happened; it is about what the substitution is
worth. Both graders see \Kf{} giving more unsolicited advice, but the held-out judge sees far more
of it ($-1.95$ acts vs.\ the primary oracle's $-0.31$), and that larger offset consumes the
flattery K=5 saves.

\paragraph{We therefore claim the substitution and not the reduction.} The primary oracle was the
training reward; the held-out judge was not, and where the two disagree we report the weaker
statement. The defensible finding at the endpoint is that look-ahead changed \emph{which} MI
violations the therapist commits, robustly and by large margins, and that whether this nets out to
fewer violations is grader-dependent.

\paragraph{A rate would have told a cleaner and less honest story.} Measured per therapist turn
rather than per session, MI-inconsistency is lower under \Kf{} by a large margin under
\emph{both} graders ($\dz{}=0.71$ primary, $0.65$ held-out, both $p<10^{-4}$). That looks like the
clean win the per-session counts refuse to give. It is a denominator effect: at iteration~10 the
\Kf{} policy takes $14.4$ therapist turns to the \Kz{} policy's $10.2$, so dividing by turns
flatters the arm that talks longer. The rate is not wrong --- a client does experience violations
per turn --- but it is not evidence of fewer violations, and reporting it alone would repeat, one
level up, the mistake of reporting the channel alone.

\paragraph{Composition, not volume.} At iteration~10 over-praise accounts for
$3.042/4.958 = 61.3\%$ of the \Kz{} policy's violations and $0.625/3.344 = 18.7\%$ of the
\Kf{} policy's; advice-without-permission accounts for $1.594/4.958 = 32.1\%$ and
$1.906/3.344 = 57.0\%$ respectively. The two policies fail in different ways. Whether one of them
fails less often is the question our two graders cannot agree on.
